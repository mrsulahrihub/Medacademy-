Chemotherapy

MedAcademy - Medical Students

1. Introduction to Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy refers to the use of chemical agents to treat infections, malignancies and certain other diseases. In pharmacology, the term is often used broadly for drugs that selectively inhibit or destroy pathogenic organisms or cancer cells.

Antimicrobial chemotherapy includes drugs against bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Anticancer chemotherapy refers to drugs used to treat malignant tumors.

High-Yield: The basic principle of antimicrobial chemotherapy is selective toxicity - the drug should damage the pathogen more than the patient.

2. Selective Toxicity

Selective toxicity means that an antimicrobial drug acts on a structure or process that is more important to the microorganism than to the human host.

Examples

  • Penicillins target bacterial cell-wall synthesis.
  • Macrolides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis.
  • Fluoroquinolones inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase/topoisomerase.
  • Antifungals may target ergosterol or fungal cell membranes.
  • Antivirals may target viral enzymes such as polymerases or proteases.

3. Major Classes of Antimicrobial Drugs

Group Major Examples

4. Penicillins

Penicillins are β-lactam antibiotics that inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis.

Mechanism

Penicillins bind penicillin-binding proteins and inhibit transpeptidation, which is necessary for peptidoglycan cross-linking.

This weakens the bacterial cell wall and can result in bacterial cell death.

Examples

  • Penicillin G
  • Penicillin V
  • Amoxicillin
  • Ampicillin
  • Oxacillin
  • Piperacillin

Important Adverse Effects

  • Allergic reactions
  • Rash
  • Anaphylaxis
  • Diarrhea
  • Clostridioides difficile infection with antibiotic exposure
Remember: Penicillins are β-lactam antibiotics and primarily inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis.

5. Cephalosporins

Cephalosporins are β-lactam antibiotics divided into generations. Their antibacterial spectrum changes across generations.

Generation Examples Important Point
1st Cefazolin, cephalexin Good Gram-positive activity; cefazolin commonly used for surgical prophylaxis
2nd Cefuroxime, cefoxitin Expanded Gram-negative activity; some agents cover anaerobes
3rd Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime Strong Gram-negative activity; ceftazidime covers Pseudomonas
4th Cefepime Broad spectrum including Pseudomonas
5th Ceftaroline Activity against MRSA

6. Carbapenems

Carbapenems are broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotics.

Examples

  • Imipenem
  • Meropenem
  • Ertapenem
  • Doripenem

They are commonly reserved for serious infections caused by resistant organisms or polymicrobial infections when appropriate.

Important Adverse Effects

  • Allergic reactions
  • Gastrointestinal effects
  • Seizure risk, particularly with imipenem in susceptible patients

7. Vancomycin

Vancomycin is a glycopeptide antibiotic that inhibits bacterial cell-wall synthesis.

Important Uses

  • Serious infections caused by susceptible Gram-positive organisms
  • MRSA infections
  • Oral vancomycin for Clostridioides difficile infection

Adverse Effects

  • Nephrotoxicity
  • Infusion-related reaction including "red man syndrome"
  • Ototoxicity is uncommon but possible
Oral vancomycin is used for intestinal C. difficile infection because it achieves high concentrations in the gut.

8. Aminoglycosides

Aminoglycosides inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding the 30S ribosomal subunit.

Examples

  • Gentamicin
  • Amikacin
  • Tobramycin
  • Streptomycin

Important Uses

  • Serious Gram-negative infections
  • Selected combination therapy
  • Specific infections such as tuberculosis for selected agents

Major Toxicities

  • Nephrotoxicity
  • Ototoxicity
  • Neuromuscular blockade
Aminoglycosides require careful dosing and monitoring because nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity can be serious.

9. Tetracyclines

Tetracyclines bind the 30S ribosomal subunit and inhibit attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA.

Examples

  • Doxycycline
  • Tetracycline
  • Minocycline

Uses

  • Chlamydial infections
  • Rickettsial infections
  • Mycoplasma infections
  • Acne
  • Selected tick-borne infections

Adverse Effects

  • Gastrointestinal irritation
  • Photosensitivity
  • Esophagitis
  • Tooth discoloration
  • Effects on developing bone and teeth
Tetracyclines are generally avoided during pregnancy and in young children when safer alternatives are appropriate.

10. Macrolides

Macrolides bind the 50S ribosomal subunit and inhibit bacterial protein synthesis.

Examples

  • Azithromycin
  • Clarithromycin
  • Erythromycin

Uses

  • Atypical respiratory infections
  • Chlamydial infections
  • Selected respiratory tract infections
  • Part of some H. pylori treatment regimens

Adverse Effects

  • Gastrointestinal upset
  • QT prolongation
  • Drug interactions, especially with some macrolides
Clarithromycin and erythromycin have more clinically important CYP3A4 inhibition than azithromycin.

11. Fluoroquinolones

Fluoroquinolones inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and/or topoisomerase IV.

Examples

  • Ciprofloxacin
  • Levofloxacin
  • Moxifloxacin

Uses

  • Selected complicated bacterial infections
  • Some urinary tract infections
  • Selected respiratory infections
  • Ciprofloxacin has activity against Pseudomonas

Adverse Effects

  • Tendinitis and tendon rupture
  • Peripheral neuropathy
  • CNS effects
  • QT prolongation with some agents
  • Dysglycemia

12. Sulfonamides and Trimethoprim

These drugs interfere with bacterial folate metabolism.

Sulfonamides

Sulfonamides inhibit dihydropteroate synthase.

Trimethoprim

Trimethoprim inhibits bacterial dihydrofolate reductase.

Co-trimoxazole

Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole blocks two sequential steps in folate synthesis.

Adverse Effects

  • Rash
  • Hypersensitivity reactions
  • Hyperkalemia with trimethoprim
  • Bone marrow suppression
  • Hemolysis in susceptible patients

13. Metronidazole

Metronidazole is activated in susceptible anaerobic organisms and certain protozoa, producing reactive metabolites that damage DNA.

Uses

  • Anaerobic bacterial infections
  • Clostridioides difficile - selected treatment situations depend on guidelines
  • Trichomoniasis
  • Giardiasis
  • Amebiasis

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea
  • Metallic taste
  • Peripheral neuropathy with prolonged use
  • Dark urine may occur

14. Antitubercular Drugs

Tuberculosis is treated using combinations of drugs to prevent treatment failure and resistance.

Major First-Line Drugs

  • Isoniazid (INH)
  • Rifampin
  • Pyrazinamide
  • Ethambutol

Isoniazid

Inhibits synthesis of mycolic acids.

Important adverse effects include hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy. Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is commonly given to reduce the risk of neuropathy in patients at risk.

Rifampin

Inhibits bacterial DNA-dependent RNA polymerase.

It is a potent enzyme inducer and can cause orange-red discoloration of body fluids.

Pyrazinamide

Important adverse effects include hepatotoxicity and hyperuricemia.

Ethambutol

Inhibits arabinosyl transferase. The major classic toxicity is optic neuritis, which can affect visual acuity and color vision.

Exam Tip: Remember TB toxicity: INH → liver + neuropathy, Rifampin → enzyme induction + orange fluids, Pyrazinamide → liver + hyperuricemia, Ethambutol → optic toxicity.

15. Antifungal Drugs

Major Classes

  • Azoles
  • Polyenes
  • Echinocandins
  • Allylamines
  • Other antifungal agents

Azoles

Azoles inhibit fungal ergosterol synthesis.

Examples include fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole and ketoconazole.

Amphotericin B

Amphotericin B binds ergosterol and forms membrane pores.

Important adverse effects include infusion reactions, nephrotoxicity and electrolyte abnormalities.

Echinocandins

Examples include caspofungin, micafungin and anidulafungin. They inhibit β-1,3-D-glucan synthesis in the fungal cell wall.

High-Yield: Amphotericin B → binds ergosterol. Azoles → inhibit ergosterol synthesis. Echinocandins → inhibit fungal cell-wall glucan synthesis.

16. Antiviral Drugs

Antiviral drugs target specific steps in the viral life cycle.

Anti-Herpes Drugs

  • Acyclovir
  • Valacyclovir
  • Ganciclovir

Acyclovir inhibits viral DNA polymerase after activation by viral thymidine kinase in susceptible herpes viruses.

Influenza Drugs

  • Oseltamivir
  • Zanamivir

These are neuraminidase inhibitors used for selected influenza infections and prevention according to current recommendations.

Antiretroviral Therapy

HIV treatment uses combinations of antiretroviral drugs from different classes to suppress viral replication and reduce resistance.

Major HIV Drug Classes

  • NRTIs
  • NNRTIs
  • Protease inhibitors
  • Integrase inhibitors
  • Entry/fusion inhibitors
  • Capsid-targeting agents
Combination antiretroviral therapy is used rather than relying on a single drug because combination therapy improves viral suppression and helps limit resistance.

17. Antiparasitic Drugs

Important Examples

  • Albendazole - many helminthic infections
  • Mebendazole - intestinal nematodes
  • Ivermectin - selected nematode and ectoparasite infections
  • Praziquantel - many trematode and cestode infections
  • Metronidazole - selected protozoal infections
  • Artemisinin-based therapies - malaria treatment

18. Antimicrobial Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance occurs when microorganisms develop the ability to survive exposure to drugs that previously inhibited or killed them.

Mechanisms of Resistance

  • Drug-inactivating enzymes
  • Alteration of drug target
  • Reduced drug entry
  • Increased drug efflux
  • Alternative metabolic pathways
  • Biofilm formation

Examples

  • β-lactamase production
  • Altered penicillin-binding proteins
  • Altered ribosomal targets
  • Reduced membrane permeability

Prevention

  • Use antibiotics only when indicated
  • Choose appropriate drug and dose
  • Use appropriate duration according to current guidelines
  • Improve infection prevention and control
  • Use culture and susceptibility testing when appropriate

19. Anticancer Chemotherapy

Anticancer chemotherapy uses drugs that kill cancer cells or inhibit their growth. Many conventional cytotoxic drugs preferentially affect rapidly dividing cells.

Major Classes

  • Alkylating agents
  • Antimetabolites
  • Antitumor antibiotics
  • Microtubule inhibitors
  • Topoisomerase inhibitors
  • Platinum compounds
  • Hormonal therapies
  • Targeted therapies
  • Immunotherapies

20. Alkylating Agents

Alkylating agents damage DNA by forming covalent bonds with DNA bases. This interferes with DNA replication and transcription.

Examples

  • Cyclophosphamide
  • Ifosfamide
  • Busulfan
  • Temozolomide

Important Toxicities

  • Bone marrow suppression
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Infertility
  • Secondary malignancies with some agents

Cyclophosphamide

Cyclophosphamide can cause hemorrhagic cystitis because of the metabolite acrolein.

Mesna can be used to reduce the risk of urothelial toxicity in appropriate high-risk regimens.

21. Antimetabolites

Antimetabolites interfere with nucleotide synthesis or function. They are particularly active against rapidly dividing cells.

Methotrexate

Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and reduces tetrahydrofolate availability.

It is used in several cancers and also in non-malignant conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis.

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU)

5-FU inhibits thymidylate synthase through an active metabolite and interferes with DNA synthesis.

Cytarabine

Cytarabine is a pyrimidine analog that inhibits DNA synthesis and is especially important in acute myeloid leukemia regimens.

Adverse Effects

  • Myelosuppression
  • Mucositis
  • Gastrointestinal toxicity
  • Specific organ toxicities depending on the drug

22. Antitumor Antibiotics

Doxorubicin

Doxorubicin intercalates DNA and inhibits topoisomerase II while also generating free radicals.

Major Toxicity

Dose-related cardiomyopathy is a classic toxicity.

Bleomycin

Bleomycin causes DNA strand breaks through free-radical mechanisms.

The classic dose-limiting toxicity is pulmonary toxicity.

Remember: Doxorubicin → cardiotoxicity. Bleomycin → pulmonary toxicity.

23. Platinum Compounds

Cisplatin

Cisplatin forms DNA cross-links, preventing DNA replication and transcription.

Major Adverse Effects

  • Nephrotoxicity
  • Ototoxicity
  • Peripheral neuropathy
  • Severe nausea and vomiting

Carboplatin

Carboplatin has a similar mechanism but generally causes more myelosuppression and less nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity than cisplatin.

24. Microtubule Inhibitors

Vinca Alkaloids

  • Vincristine
  • Vinblastine

Vinca alkaloids inhibit microtubule polymerization.

Important Toxicities

Vincristine is particularly associated with peripheral neuropathy. Vinblastine causes more prominent bone marrow suppression.

Taxanes

  • Paclitaxel
  • Docetaxel

Taxanes stabilize microtubules and prevent their normal depolymerization.

Adverse Effects

  • Peripheral neuropathy
  • Myelosuppression
  • Hypersensitivity reactions with some formulations

25. Topoisomerase Inhibitors

Topoisomerase I

Irinotecan and topotecan inhibit topoisomerase I.

Irinotecan is particularly associated with diarrhea and myelosuppression.

Topoisomerase II

Etoposide inhibits topoisomerase II.

Doxorubicin also has topoisomerase II inhibitory activity.

26. Hormonal Anticancer Therapy

Some cancers depend on hormones for growth. Hormonal therapy blocks hormone production or action.

Examples

  • Tamoxifen - selective estrogen receptor modulator
  • Anastrozole - aromatase inhibitor
  • Letrozole - aromatase inhibitor
  • Flutamide - androgen receptor antagonist
  • Bicalutamide - androgen receptor antagonist
  • Enzalutamide - androgen receptor inhibitor

Tamoxifen

Tamoxifen blocks estrogen receptors in breast tissue but has partial agonist effects in some other tissues.

27. Targeted Anticancer Therapy

Targeted therapies act on specific molecular pathways that are important for cancer-cell survival or growth.

Examples

  • Imatinib - BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase inhibitor
  • Trastuzumab - HER2-targeted monoclonal antibody
  • Rituximab - CD20-targeted monoclonal antibody
  • Bevacizumab - VEGF-targeted therapy

Important Concept

Targeted therapies can be more selective than conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, but they still have important toxicities and resistance can develop.

28. Cancer Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy stimulates or enhances the immune system's ability to recognize and attack cancer cells.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors

  • Pembrolizumab
  • Nivolumab
  • Atezolizumab
  • Ipilimumab

These drugs block inhibitory immune pathways such as PD-1, PD-L1 or CTLA-4.

Important Adverse Effects

Immune-related adverse effects may involve organs such as the skin, bowel, liver, lungs and endocrine glands.

29. Anticancer Drug Resistance

Cancer cells can develop resistance to chemotherapy through multiple mechanisms.

  • Increased drug efflux
  • Altered drug targets
  • Enhanced DNA repair
  • Reduced drug activation
  • Increased drug inactivation
  • Changes in apoptotic pathways
Combination chemotherapy may be used because drugs with different mechanisms can improve cancer-cell killing and reduce the likelihood that a single resistance mechanism will cause complete treatment failure.

30. Common Toxicities of Cytotoxic Chemotherapy

  • Myelosuppression
  • Neutropenia and infection risk
  • Anemia
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Mucositis
  • Alopecia
  • Infertility
  • Organ-specific toxicity

Tumor Lysis Syndrome

Rapid destruction of tumor cells can release intracellular contents into the circulation. This may cause hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia and can lead to acute kidney injury and cardiac complications.

Prevention and management may include hydration and urate-lowering therapy such as allopurinol or rasburicase in appropriate high-risk patients.

31. Important Questions and Answers

Q1. What is chemotherapy?

Chemotherapy is the use of chemical drugs to treat disease, particularly infections and malignancies.

Q2. What is selective toxicity?

Selective toxicity means that a drug preferentially harms the pathogen or abnormal cell while causing less damage to the host.

Q3. What is the mechanism of penicillins?

Penicillins inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis by binding penicillin-binding proteins and interfering with peptidoglycan cross-linking.

Q4. Which antibiotic inhibits the 30S ribosomal subunit and can cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity?

Aminoglycosides such as gentamicin and amikacin.

Q5. What is the major toxicity of vancomycin that is classically tested?

Nephrotoxicity is important, and rapid IV infusion can cause an infusion reaction known as red man syndrome.

Q6. What is the mechanism of fluoroquinolones?

They inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and/or topoisomerase IV.

Q7. What are the four major first-line drugs for tuberculosis?

Isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol.

Q8. What is the classic toxicity of ethambutol?

Optic neuritis with impairment of visual acuity and color vision.

Q9. What is the major toxicity of isoniazid?

Hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy are important toxicities.

Q10. Which TB drug causes orange-red discoloration of body fluids?

Rifampin.

Q11. What is the mechanism of amphotericin B?

It binds ergosterol in fungal cell membranes and forms membrane pores.

Q12. What is the mechanism of azole antifungals?

They inhibit fungal ergosterol synthesis.

Q13. What is the mechanism of methotrexate?

Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase and interferes with folate-dependent nucleotide synthesis.

Q14. Which anticancer drug is associated with cardiomyopathy?

Doxorubicin is classically associated with dose-related cardiomyopathy.

Q15. Which anticancer drug is associated with pulmonary toxicity?

Bleomycin is classically associated with pulmonary toxicity.

Q16. Which anticancer drug causes hemorrhagic cystitis?

Cyclophosphamide can cause hemorrhagic cystitis due to the metabolite acrolein.

Q17. What is the classic toxicity of cisplatin?

Nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity and peripheral neuropathy are important toxicities.

Q18. What is the major toxicity of vincristine?

Peripheral neuropathy is the classic dose-limiting toxicity of vincristine.

Q19. What is tumor lysis syndrome?

It is a metabolic emergency caused by rapid destruction of tumor cells, leading to release of intracellular contents and abnormalities such as hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia and hyperuricemia.

Q20. What is antimicrobial resistance?

It is the ability of microorganisms to survive or multiply despite exposure to an antimicrobial drug that would normally inhibit or kill them.

32. Chemotherapy MCQs

1. The main principle of antimicrobial chemotherapy is:

A. Maximum toxicity to host

B. Selective toxicity

C. Complete immune suppression

D. Permanent bacterial inhibition

Answer: B. Selective toxicity

2. Penicillins primarily inhibit:

A. DNA replication

B. Protein synthesis

C. Cell-wall synthesis

D. RNA synthesis

Answer: C. Cell-wall synthesis

3. Aminoglycosides bind primarily to:

A. 30S ribosomal subunit

B. 50S ribosomal subunit

C. DNA gyrase

D. Cell wall

Answer: A. 30S ribosomal subunit

4. Which antibiotic is associated with nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity?

A. Amoxicillin

B. Gentamicin

C. Azithromycin

D. Doxycycline

Answer: B. Gentamicin

A. Vancomycin

A. DNA gyrase/topoisomerase

A. Ciprofloxacin

B. Penicillin

8. Which TB drug causes optic neuritis?

A. Isoniazid
