🔥 Inflammation

Complete MBBS-Level Pathology Study Page

1. Introduction to Inflammation

Inflammation is a protective response of vascularized tissues to infection, tissue injury, or other harmful stimuli. Its main purpose is to eliminate the injurious agent, remove damaged tissue, and initiate repair.

High-Yield: Inflammation is protective, but excessive or uncontrolled inflammation can itself cause tissue injury.

Five Cardinal Signs

  • Rubor — Redness
  • Calor — Heat
  • Tumor — Swelling
  • Dolor — Pain
  • Functio laesa — Loss of function

4. Causes of Inflammation

  • Microbial infections
  • Tissue necrosis
  • Physical agents such as trauma, burns and radiation
  • Chemical irritants and toxins
  • Foreign bodies
  • Immune reactions
  • Autoimmune diseases
  • Crystal deposition such as gout

5. Acute Inflammation

Acute inflammation is a rapid response that delivers leukocytes and plasma proteins to the site of injury.

Major Components

  1. Vasodilation
  2. Increased vascular permeability
  3. Exudation of plasma proteins
  4. Leukocyte recruitment
  5. Leukocyte activation
  6. Removal of offending agent

6. Vascular Changes in Acute Inflammation

Step 1 — Vasodilation

Arteriolar vasodilation increases blood flow to the affected area. This produces redness and heat.

Step 2 — Increased Vascular Permeability

Endothelial gaps and other permeability mechanisms allow protein-rich fluid to leave the blood vessels.

Step 3 — Edema

Accumulation of protein-rich extravascular fluid produces inflammatory edema.

Important: Exudate is protein-rich inflammatory fluid, whereas transudate is usually a low-protein fluid caused mainly by hydrostatic or osmotic imbalance.

9. Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis is the process by which neutrophils and macrophages recognize, engulf, and destroy microorganisms or cellular debris.

Three Major Steps

  1. Recognition and attachment
  2. Engulfment
  3. Killing and degradation

Important Opsonins

  • IgG
  • C3b
  • Collectins such as mannose-binding lectin

10. Neutrophils

Neutrophils are the predominant leukocytes during the early phase of most acute inflammatory responses.

Important Functions

  • Phagocytosis
  • Microbial killing
  • Release of reactive oxygen species
  • Release of lysosomal enzymes
  • Formation of neutrophil extracellular traps in appropriate settings

11. Chronic Inflammation

Chronic inflammation is prolonged inflammation in which inflammation, tissue injury, and attempts at repair may occur simultaneously.

Major Causes

  • Persistent infections
  • Autoimmune diseases
  • Prolonged exposure to toxic agents
  • Some metabolic disorders

Main Cells

  • Macrophages
  • Lymphocytes
  • Plasma cells
  • Eosinophils in selected conditions

Important Features

  • Persistent inflammatory infiltrate
  • Ongoing tissue destruction
  • Angiogenesis
  • Fibrosis

12. Macrophages

Macrophages are central cells in chronic inflammation and also play important roles in acute inflammation and tissue repair.

Functions

  • Phagocytosis
  • Antigen presentation
  • Cytokine production
  • Microbial killing
  • Growth factor production
  • Participation in tissue repair and fibrosis

13. Granulomatous Inflammation

Granulomatous inflammation is a distinctive form of chronic inflammation characterized by collections of activated macrophages, often called epithelioid cells.

Common Causes

  • Tuberculosis
  • Fungal infections
  • Sarcoidosis
  • Foreign-body reactions
  • Selected immune-mediated diseases
MBBS Point: A granuloma is a localized collection of activated macrophages, often surrounded by lymphocytes.

14. Morphologic Patterns of Acute Inflammation

Serous Inflammation

Thin, relatively cell-poor fluid accumulation. Example: skin blister after a burn.

Fibrinous Inflammation

Fibrin-rich exudate. Commonly seen on serosal surfaces in certain injuries.

Suppurative / Purulent Inflammation

Production of pus containing neutrophils, necrotic debris, and edema fluid.

Ulcer

A local defect of the surface epithelium caused by shedding of inflamed necrotic tissue.

15. Systemic Effects of Inflammation

  • Fever
  • Leukocytosis
  • Acute-phase protein production
  • Malaise and fatigue
  • Tachycardia
  • Severe inflammation may contribute to hypotension and shock

Important Acute-Phase Proteins

  • C-reactive protein (CRP)
  • Serum amyloid A
  • Fibrinogen
Exam Point: IL-6 is an important cytokine stimulating hepatic acute-phase protein production.

18. MBBS-Level Questions & Answers

Q1. Define inflammation.

Inflammation is a protective response of vascularized tissue to injury or infection that brings immune cells, plasma proteins, and mediators to the affected site.

Q2. What are the five cardinal signs of inflammation?

Rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, and loss of function.

Q3. What is acute inflammation?

A rapid inflammatory response characterized by vascular changes, plasma protein exudation, and predominantly neutrophilic leukocyte infiltration.

Q4. What is chronic inflammation?

Prolonged inflammation characterized by macrophages, lymphocytes, and other mononuclear cells, with tissue injury and attempts at repair.

Q5. Which leukocyte predominates in acute inflammation?

Neutrophils.

Q6. Which cells predominate in chronic inflammation?

Macrophages and lymphocytes, with plasma cells in many settings.

Q7. What is chemotaxis?

Directed movement of leukocytes toward a chemical concentration gradient at the site of inflammation.

Q8. Name important chemotactic agents.

C5a, LTB4, chemokines, and certain bacterial products.

Q9. What is the role of histamine?

Histamine causes vasodilation and increases vascular permeability during acute inflammation.

Q10. What is the role of C3b?

C3b acts mainly as an opsonin and facilitates phagocytosis.

Q11. What is the role of C5a?

C5a promotes chemotaxis and activation of leukocytes.

Q12. What is an exudate?

An inflammatory extravascular fluid that is relatively rich in protein and often contains leukocytes.

Q13. What is an opsonin?

A molecule that coats microbes or particles and facilitates their recognition and phagocytosis by leukocytes.

Q14. Name important opsonins.

IgG and C3b are major examples.

Q15. What is granulomatous inflammation?

A form of chronic inflammation characterized by collections of activated macrophages, often with surrounding lymphocytes.

Q16. What is the difference between exudate and transudate?

Exudate is protein-rich inflammatory fluid, while transudate is usually low in protein and results mainly from hydrostatic or osmotic imbalance.

Q17. What causes fever during inflammation?

Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1 and TNF promote prostaglandin production in the hypothalamic thermoregulatory pathway, producing an increased temperature set point.

Q18. What are the possible outcomes of acute inflammation?

Resolution, suppuration/abscess formation, fibrosis/scarring, or progression to chronic inflammation.

Q19. Why is chronic inflammation associated with fibrosis?

Persistent inflammatory mediators and growth factors stimulate fibroblasts and extracellular matrix deposition during attempts at repair.

Q20. What are the five Rs of inflammation?

Recognition, Recruitment, Removal, Regulation, and Resolution.

21. Test Paper Answer Key

MCQs: 1-B, 2-A, 3-B, 4-B, 5-A, 6-A, 7-A, 8-A, 9-A, 10-A

Short Question Key Points

  • Inflammation = protective vascularized tissue response to injury/infection.
  • Five signs = rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, loss of function.
  • Acute = rapid, neutrophil predominant; chronic = prolonged, macrophage/lymphocyte predominant.
  • Chemotaxis = directed leukocyte movement toward chemical signals.
  • C3b = opsonization.
  • C5a = chemotaxis and leukocyte activation.
  • Exudate = protein-rich inflammatory extravascular fluid.
  • Recruitment = margination, rolling, adhesion, transmigration and chemotaxis.

22. Final High-Yield Revision

  • Acute inflammation → predominantly neutrophils.
  • Chronic inflammation → macrophages and lymphocytes.
  • Rolling → selectins.
  • Firm adhesion → integrins.
  • Chemotaxis → C5a, LTB4, chemokines.
  • Opsonization → C3b and IgG.
  • Histamine → vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.
  • Bradykinin → pain and vascular permeability.
  • Prostaglandins → pain, fever and vasodilation.
  • IL-6 → hepatic acute-phase protein production.
  • Granuloma → activated macrophages/epithelioid cells.
  • Possible outcomes → resolution, suppuration, fibrosis or chronic inflammation.
