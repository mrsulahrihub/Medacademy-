Drugs Affecting Blood

Hematinics • Anticoagulants • Thrombolytics • Antiplatelet Drugs

1. Introduction to Drugs Affecting Blood

Drugs affecting blood are used to prevent or treat anemia, thrombosis, embolism and other disorders involving blood cells and hemostasis.

Major Groups

  • Hematinics
  • Anticoagulants
  • Thrombolytic drugs
  • Antiplatelet drugs
  • Other drugs affecting coagulation and blood formation
High-Yield: Hematinics help form blood cells, anticoagulants prevent or limit clot formation, thrombolytics dissolve established clots, and antiplatelet drugs reduce platelet aggregation.

2. Hematinics

Hematinics are substances required for normal formation of red blood cells and hemoglobin.

Main Hematinics

  • Iron
  • Folic acid
  • Vitamin B12
  • Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents

Important Functions

  • Iron is required for hemoglobin synthesis.
  • Vitamin B12 is required for DNA synthesis and neurological function.
  • Folate is required for DNA synthesis and normal cell division.
  • Erythropoietin stimulates red blood cell production.
Exam Point: Iron deficiency usually causes microcytic hypochromic anemia, whereas vitamin B12 and folate deficiency usually cause megaloblastic anemia.

3. Iron Preparations

Iron is essential for hemoglobin synthesis and normal erythropoiesis. Iron deficiency is a common cause of microcytic hypochromic anemia.

Oral Iron Preparations

  • Ferrous sulfate
  • Ferrous fumarate
  • Ferrous gluconate

Mechanism

Iron provides elemental iron required for hemoglobin synthesis and red blood cell production.

Advantages of Oral Iron

  • Inexpensive
  • Convenient
  • Effective in many patients
  • Can be given for long-term replacement

Adverse Effects

  • Nausea
  • Abdominal discomfort
  • Constipation
  • Diarrhea
  • Dark stools
Vitamin C can enhance iron absorption, while some foods and medications can reduce absorption.

Intravenous Iron

IV iron is used when oral iron is ineffective, poorly tolerated, poorly absorbed, or when rapid iron replacement is appropriate.

Examples

  • Iron sucrose
  • Ferric carboxymaltose
  • Ferric derisomaltose

IV Iron Adverse Effects

  • Infusion reactions
  • Hypersensitivity reactions
  • Injection-site reactions depending on preparation

4. Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12 is required for DNA synthesis and normal neurological function. Its deficiency can produce megaloblastic anemia and neurological abnormalities.

Preparations

  • Cyanocobalamin
  • Hydroxocobalamin

Causes of B12 Deficiency

  • Pernicious anemia
  • Malabsorption
  • Gastric surgery
  • Strict vegan diet without adequate supplementation
  • Gastrointestinal disorders

Clinical Features

  • Megaloblastic anemia
  • Glossitis
  • Peripheral neuropathy
  • Loss of vibration and position sense
  • Cognitive changes in some patients
Important: B12 deficiency can cause neurological damage. Folic acid may improve the anemia while neurological injury from untreated B12 deficiency continues to progress.

Laboratory Findings

  • Increased methylmalonic acid
  • Increased homocysteine
  • Macrocytosis
  • Megaloblastic changes in the blood and bone marrow

5. Folic Acid

Folic acid is essential for DNA synthesis and red blood cell production. Folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia.

Uses

  • Folate-deficiency anemia
  • Pregnancy supplementation to reduce neural tube defects
  • Prevention of folate deficiency in selected patients

Causes of Folate Deficiency

  • Poor dietary intake
  • Malabsorption
  • Alcohol use disorder
  • Increased requirements during pregnancy
  • Some medications

Vitamin B12 vs Folate Deficiency

Feature Vitamin B12 Deficiency Folate Deficiency
Anemia Megaloblastic Megaloblastic
Neurological manifestations May occur and can be significant Not typical
Homocysteine Increased Increased
Methylmalonic acid Increased Usually normal

6. Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents stimulate red blood cell production through activation of erythropoietin receptors.

Examples

  • Epoetin alfa
  • Darbepoetin alfa

Uses

  • Anemia associated with chronic kidney disease
  • Selected chemotherapy-associated anemia according to clinical criteria

Mechanism

ESAs stimulate erythroid precursor cells in the bone marrow, increasing red blood cell production.

Adverse Effects

  • Hypertension
  • Thromboembolic complications
  • Headache
ESAs should be used according to specific clinical indications and target hemoglobin levels because excessive correction can increase cardiovascular and thrombotic risk.

7. Anticoagulants

Anticoagulants reduce the formation or extension of fibrin clots. They do not directly dissolve an established clot.

Major Groups

  • Unfractionated heparin
  • Low-molecular-weight heparins
  • Warfarin
  • Direct oral anticoagulants
Remember: Anticoagulants prevent or limit clot propagation. Thrombolytics actively break down fibrin in selected established thrombi.

8. Unfractionated Heparin

Unfractionated heparin acts by enhancing antithrombin activity, which inhibits several activated coagulation factors.

Major Targets

  • Thrombin (Factor IIa)
  • Factor Xa
  • Other activated clotting factors

Uses

  • Acute venous thromboembolism
  • Acute coronary syndromes in selected patients
  • Periprocedural anticoagulation
  • Hemodialysis and selected extracorporeal circuits
  • Selected bridging situations

Advantages

  • Rapid onset when given parenterally
  • Short half-life
  • Can be used during pregnancy when anticoagulation is indicated
  • Protamine can neutralize much of its anticoagulant effect

Adverse Effects

  • Bleeding
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)
  • Osteoporosis with prolonged use
HIT: Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is an immune-mediated, prothrombotic reaction associated with a fall in platelet count and increased risk of thrombosis.

9. Low-Molecular-Weight Heparins

LMWHs enhance antithrombin activity and predominantly inhibit Factor Xa, with less effect on thrombin compared with unfractionated heparin.

Examples

  • Enoxaparin
  • Dalteparin
  • Tinzaparin

Advantages

  • More predictable anticoagulant response
  • Longer duration than unfractionated heparin
  • Usually no routine laboratory monitoring is required
  • Convenient subcutaneous administration

Important Point

Dose adjustment or alternative therapy may be required in significant renal impairment.

10. Warfarin

Warfarin is an oral anticoagulant that inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase. This decreases activation of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

Vitamin K-Dependent Factors

  • Factor II
  • Factor VII
  • Factor IX
  • Factor X
  • Protein C
  • Protein S

Mechanism

Vitamin K epoxide reductase inhibition → reduced vitamin K recycling → reduced activation of vitamin K-dependent clotting proteins.

Monitoring

Warfarin therapy is monitored using PT/INR.

Adverse Effects

  • Bleeding
  • Skin necrosis in rare situations
  • Many drug interactions
  • Dietary vitamin K interactions
  • Fetal toxicity

Reversal

  • Vitamin K
  • Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) when urgent reversal is required
Warfarin is generally contraindicated during pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and can cause fetal harm.

11. Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

DOACs directly inhibit specific coagulation factors and are widely used for many indications including venous thromboembolism and prevention of stroke in selected patients with non-valvular atrial fibrillation.

Direct Factor Xa Inhibitors

  • Apixaban
  • Rivaroxaban
  • Edoxaban

Direct Thrombin Inhibitor

  • Dabigatran

Advantages

  • Rapid onset
  • Fixed dosing in many situations
  • No routine INR monitoring
  • Fewer dietary interactions than warfarin

Important Considerations

  • Renal function may affect dosing and selection
  • Drug interactions can occur
  • Bleeding remains the major adverse effect
  • Not all DOACs are appropriate for every clinical situation

Reversal Agents

  • Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran
  • Andexanet alfa can reverse the effect of certain Factor Xa inhibitors in selected settings

13. Thrombolytic Drugs

Thrombolytic drugs, also called fibrinolytics, activate plasminogen to form plasmin, which breaks down fibrin within thrombi.

Major Drugs

  • Alteplase
  • Tenecteplase
  • Reteplase

Mechanism

Plasminogen → Plasmin → Fibrin degradation

Clinical Uses

  • Selected acute ischemic stroke patients within appropriate treatment windows
  • Selected STEMI patients when appropriate reperfusion by PCI is unavailable or unsuitable
  • Massive/high-risk pulmonary embolism in selected patients
  • Other selected acute thrombotic emergencies

Major Adverse Effect

The most important adverse effect is serious bleeding, especially intracranial hemorrhage.

Thrombolytic therapy requires careful assessment for contraindications because serious bleeding can be life-threatening.

14. Alteplase

Alteplase is recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). It converts plasminogen to plasmin and promotes fibrin breakdown.

Important Uses

  • Acute ischemic stroke in eligible patients
  • Selected myocardial infarction situations
  • Selected pulmonary embolism
  • Catheter clearance in selected formulations and uses

Major Risks

  • Major bleeding
  • Intracranial hemorrhage

15. Tenecteplase

Tenecteplase is a genetically modified tPA derivative with greater fibrin specificity and a longer half-life than alteplase.

Important Features

  • Longer half-life than alteplase
  • More fibrin-specific than native tPA
  • Can be administered as a bolus in appropriate indications
  • Used in selected acute myocardial infarction settings
  • Used in appropriate acute ischemic stroke protocols in some settings

16. Antiplatelet Drugs

Antiplatelet drugs reduce platelet activation and aggregation. They are especially important in arterial thrombosis, including coronary and cerebrovascular disease.

Major Groups

  • Aspirin
  • P2Y12 receptor inhibitors
  • Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors
  • Phosphodiesterase inhibitors

17. Aspirin

Aspirin irreversibly inhibits cyclooxygenase in platelets, reducing thromboxane A2 production and therefore platelet aggregation.

Mechanism

COX inhibition → ↓ TXA2 → ↓ Platelet aggregation

Clinical Uses

  • Secondary prevention of cardiovascular events in appropriate patients
  • Acute coronary syndromes
  • Selected cerebrovascular disease

Adverse Effects

  • Gastrointestinal irritation
  • Gastrointestinal bleeding
  • Hypersensitivity
  • Bronchospasm in susceptible individuals
High-Yield: Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet COX, and the antiplatelet effect lasts for the life of the affected platelet.

18. P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitors

Examples

  • Clopidogrel
  • Prasugrel
  • Ticagrelor
  • Cangrelor

These drugs inhibit ADP-mediated platelet activation through the P2Y12 receptor.

Clopidogrel

Clopidogrel is an irreversible P2Y12 inhibitor and is a prodrug that requires hepatic activation.

Prasugrel

Prasugrel is a potent irreversible P2Y12 inhibitor. It is generally avoided in patients with a prior stroke or TIA because of increased bleeding risk.

Ticagrelor

Ticagrelor is a reversible P2Y12 inhibitor.

Cangrelor

Cangrelor is an intravenous, rapidly acting and reversible P2Y12 inhibitor.

Major Adverse Effect

Bleeding is the major adverse effect of P2Y12 inhibitors.

19. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa Inhibitors

Examples

  • Abciximab
  • Eptifibatide
  • Tirofiban

Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa is the final common pathway for platelet aggregation. Blocking this receptor produces powerful inhibition of platelet aggregation.

Uses

These drugs are mainly used in selected high-risk acute coronary intervention settings.

Adverse Effects

  • Bleeding
  • Thrombocytopenia

20. Other Antiplatelet Drugs

Dipyridamole

Dipyridamole inhibits platelet phosphodiesterase and increases platelet cAMP. It is used in selected cerebrovascular prevention strategies, often in combination with aspirin.

Cilostazol

Cilostazol is a PDE-3 inhibitor that increases platelet cAMP and causes vasodilation. It is used mainly for intermittent claudication.

Cilostazol is generally avoided in patients with heart failure because of its PDE-3 inhibitor class-related safety concerns.

24. Important Questions & Answers - Hematinics

Q1. What are hematinics?

Hematinics are substances required for normal red blood cell formation and hemoglobin synthesis, including iron, folate and vitamin B12.

Q2. What type of anemia is commonly caused by iron deficiency?

Iron deficiency typically causes microcytic hypochromic anemia.

Q3. What are common adverse effects of oral iron?

Nausea, abdominal discomfort, constipation or diarrhea and dark stools.

Q4. What is the important difference between B12 and folate deficiency?

Both can cause megaloblastic anemia, but vitamin B12 deficiency can cause important neurological manifestations.

Q5. Which vitamin deficiency causes increased methylmalonic acid?

Vitamin B12 deficiency.

Q6. Give examples of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents.

Epoetin alfa and darbepoetin alfa.

25. Important Questions & Answers - Anticoagulants

Q1. What is the main action of anticoagulants?

They reduce coagulation and formation or extension of fibrin clots.

Q2. How does heparin work?

Heparin enhances antithrombin activity, increasing inhibition of thrombin and Factor Xa.

Q3. What is HIT?

HIT is an immune-mediated reaction to heparin that causes platelet activation, thrombocytopenia and an increased risk of thrombosis.

Q4. How is warfarin monitored?

Warfarin is monitored using PT/INR.

Q5. Which clotting factors are affected by warfarin?

Factors II, VII, IX and X, as well as proteins C and S.

Q6. What is the mechanism of DOACs?

They directly inhibit specific coagulation factors, such as Factor Xa or thrombin.

Q7. Name three Factor Xa inhibitors.

Apixaban, rivaroxaban and edoxaban.

Q8. Which DOAC is a direct thrombin inhibitor?

Dabigatran.

26. Important Questions & Answers - Thrombolytics

Q1. What are thrombolytic drugs?

They are drugs that promote conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, leading to degradation of fibrin in thrombi.

Q2. Give examples of thrombolytic drugs.

Alteplase, tenecteplase and reteplase.

Q3. What is the major adverse effect of thrombolytics?

Serious bleeding, including intracranial hemorrhage.

Q4. What is alteplase?

Alteplase is recombinant tissue plasminogen activator that converts plasminogen to plasmin.

Q5. What is tenecteplase?

Tenecteplase is a genetically modified tPA derivative with a longer half-life and increased fibrin specificity compared with alteplase.

27. Important Questions & Answers - Antiplatelet Drugs

Q1. How does aspirin inhibit platelets?

Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet cyclooxygenase and decreases thromboxane A2 production.

Q2. What is the target of clopidogrel?

Clopidogrel inhibits the platelet P2Y12 ADP receptor.

Q3. What is the final common pathway of platelet aggregation?

The glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor is the final common pathway for platelet fibrinogen-mediated aggregation.

Q4. Give examples of GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors.

Abciximab, eptifibatide and tirofiban.

Q5. What is the major adverse effect of antiplatelet drugs?

Bleeding.

30. High-Yield Exam Revision

  • Iron: treatment of iron-deficiency anemia.
  • Vitamin B12: megaloblastic anemia + neurological manifestations.
  • Folic acid: megaloblastic anemia without typical neurological disease.
  • Epoetin alfa: stimulates erythropoiesis.
  • Heparin: enhances antithrombin.
  • HIT: important immune-mediated complication of heparin.
  • LMWH: predominantly inhibits Factor Xa through antithrombin.
  • Warfarin: inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase.
  • Warfarin monitoring: PT/INR.
  • Apixaban/Rivaroxaban/Edoxaban: Factor Xa inhibitors.
  • Dabigatran: direct thrombin inhibitor.
  • Idarucizumab: reversal of dabigatran.
  • Alteplase: recombinant tPA.
  • Thrombolytics: convert plasminogen to plasmin.
  • Aspirin: irreversible platelet COX inhibition → ↓TXA2.
  • Clopidogrel: P2Y12 inhibitor.
  • Ticagrelor: reversible P2Y12 inhibitor.
  • GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors: powerful inhibition of platelet aggregation.
  • Major common risk: bleeding.
