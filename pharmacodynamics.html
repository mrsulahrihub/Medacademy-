7. Dose–Response Relationship

The dose–response relationship describes how the magnitude of a biological response changes as the dose or concentration of a drug increases. It is an important concept in pharmacodynamics because it helps explain drug potency, efficacy and safety.

Basic Principle

In general, increasing the dose of a drug increases its effect until a maximum response is reached. Beyond this point, further increases in dose may produce little or no additional therapeutic benefit while increasing the risk of adverse effects.

Key Concept:

Increasing dose → Increasing response → Maximum response

After maximum response is reached, increasing the dose usually does not increase the maximum effect.

Two Important Types of Dose–Response Relationships

  • Graded dose–response: Measures the intensity or magnitude of response produced by different doses in an individual or experimental system.
  • Quantal dose–response: Measures an all-or-none response in a population, such as the percentage of patients achieving a predefined effect.

Changes in Dose–Response Curve

Several factors can change the position or shape of a dose–response curve. These changes are important for understanding drug interactions and receptor activity.

1. Increased Potency:

If a drug becomes more potent, the dose–response curve shifts to the left. A lower concentration is required to produce the same response.

Lower EC50 → Greater Potency
2. Decreased Potency:

If a drug becomes less potent, the curve shifts to the right. A higher concentration is required to produce the same response.

Higher EC50 → Lower Potency
3. Increased Efficacy:

Increased efficacy means that the drug can produce a greater maximum response. The maximum height of the dose–response curve increases.

Higher Emax → Greater Efficacy
4. Decreased Efficacy:

Decreased efficacy means that the drug cannot produce the same maximum response. The maximum height of the curve decreases.

Lower Emax → Lower Efficacy

Effect of a Reversible Competitive Antagonist

A reversible competitive antagonist competes with an agonist for the same receptor site.

Important:

Competitive antagonism causes a rightward shift of the dose–response curve without reducing the maximum response, provided sufficient agonist concentration can overcome the antagonism.

Right shift → Increased EC50 → Reduced apparent potency

Effect of a Non-Competitive Antagonist

A non-competitive antagonist reduces the ability of an agonist to produce its maximum response.

Exam Point:

Non-competitive antagonism generally decreases Emax, meaning the maximum response is reduced.

Effect of Partial Agonist

A partial agonist activates the receptor but produces a lower maximum response than a full agonist, even when it occupies a large proportion of available receptors.

Example:

If a full agonist produces 100% response, a partial agonist may produce a lower maximum response.

Summary of Dose–Response Changes

Change Effect on Curve EC50 Emax
Increased potency Shift to left Decreases Usually unchanged
Decreased potency Shift to right Increases Usually unchanged
Increased efficacy Higher maximum response May vary Increases
Decreased efficacy Lower maximum response May vary Decreases
Competitive antagonist Rightward shift Increases Usually unchanged
Non-competitive antagonist Reduced maximum response May vary Decreases
High-Yield Exam Rule:

Left shift = Increased potency
Right shift = Decreased potency
Higher Emax = Increased efficacy
Lower Emax = Decreased efficacy
