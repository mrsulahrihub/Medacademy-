Introduction to Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful or unpleasant response to a medicinal product that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prevention, diagnosis or treatment of disease.

Key Concept:

ADR = Harmful or unintended response to a drug occurring at normally used doses.

Adverse drug reactions are important in clinical practice because they can increase patient morbidity, prolong hospital stays and sometimes become life-threatening.

1. ADR vs Side Effect vs Medication Error

These terms are related but should not be considered identical.

Term Meaning
Adverse Drug Reaction Harmful or unintended response occurring at normally used doses.
Side Effect A secondary effect of a drug that may be expected from its pharmacological action and may be harmful, harmless or beneficial.
Medication Error A preventable mistake in prescribing, dispensing, administering or using a medication.

2. Type A Adverse Drug Reactions

Type A reactions are sometimes called Augmented reactions.

  • Usually predictable from the known pharmacological action of the drug.
  • Generally dose-dependent.
  • Often occur relatively frequently.
  • May improve after reducing the dose or stopping the drug.
Examples:
  • Hypoglycemia caused by insulin.
  • Bleeding caused by excessive anticoagulant effect.
  • Hypotension caused by antihypertensive drugs.
Exam Point: Type A = Augmented + predictable + dose-related

3. Type B Adverse Drug Reactions

Type B reactions are sometimes called Bizarre reactions.

  • Usually unpredictable from the usual pharmacological action of the drug.
  • Often not clearly dose-dependent.
  • May include allergic or idiosyncratic reactions.
  • They are generally less common than Type A reactions.
Examples:
  • Anaphylaxis caused by a drug in a susceptible person.
  • Severe hypersensitivity reaction to certain medicines.
  • Idiosyncratic drug reactions.
Exam Point: Type B = Bizarre + unpredictable + usually non-dose-dependent

4. Expanded ADR Classification: Types A–F

An expanded classification describes adverse drug reactions using six categories. The classic Rawlins and Thompson classification primarily emphasized Type A and Type B reactions.

Type Name Main Feature Example
A Augmented Predictable and dose-related Hypoglycemia from insulin
B Bizarre Unpredictable and often not dose-related Anaphylaxis
C Chronic Associated with prolonged treatment Effects associated with long-term therapy
D Delayed Appears after some time Certain drug-related cancers
E End of use Associated with withdrawal or discontinuation Withdrawal reactions
F Failure Unexpected failure of therapy Reduced effect due to drug interaction

5. Predictable and Unpredictable ADRs

Predictable ADRs

Predictable reactions are related to the known pharmacological properties of a drug.

  • Often dose-related.
  • Usually preventable or manageable.
  • Type A reactions are the classic example.

Unpredictable ADRs

Unpredictable reactions are not readily explained by the usual pharmacological action of the drug.

  • Often not dose-related.
  • May involve immune or genetic mechanisms.
  • Type B reactions are the classic example.

6. Drug Allergy and Hypersensitivity

Some adverse drug reactions are caused by immune-mediated hypersensitivity rather than the normal pharmacological action of the drug.

Possible Clinical Manifestations

  • Skin rash
  • Urticaria
  • Angioedema
  • Bronchospasm
  • Anaphylaxis
Emergency Point:

Anaphylaxis can be life-threatening and requires immediate emergency management.

7. Risk Factors for Adverse Drug Reactions

Certain patient and treatment factors increase the likelihood of adverse drug reactions.

  • Advanced age.
  • Very young age, especially in vulnerable patients.
  • Renal impairment.
  • Hepatic impairment.
  • Multiple medications (polypharmacy).
  • Previous history of drug allergy or ADR.
  • Drug interactions.
  • Genetic differences in drug metabolism or response.
  • High doses or prolonged treatment.

8. Severity of Adverse Drug Reactions

ADRs can range from mild symptoms to severe, life-threatening reactions.

Severity General Description
Mild Minor symptoms that may require little or no specific treatment.
Moderate Symptoms may require additional treatment or modification of therapy.
Severe Significant reaction that may require intensive treatment or hospitalization.
Life-threatening Reaction that can threaten the patient's life.

9. Serious Adverse Drug Reactions

A serious ADR is defined according to its outcome or consequence rather than simply how intense the symptoms feel.

Examples of Serious Outcomes

  • Death.
  • Life-threatening event.
  • Hospitalization or prolonged hospitalization.
  • Persistent or significant disability.
  • Congenital anomaly or birth defect.
  • Other medically important events requiring intervention.
Important:

"Serious" and "severe" are not exactly the same. Serious generally refers to regulatory or clinical outcome criteria, while severe describes the intensity of symptoms.

10. Drug Interactions and ADRs

Drug interactions can increase the risk of adverse reactions by changing the concentration or effect of one or more drugs.

Examples

  • One drug may increase the blood concentration of another.
  • Two drugs may produce additive effects.
  • A drug may interfere with another drug's metabolism.
  • A combination may increase the risk of bleeding, sedation or other adverse effects.

11. Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities concerned with the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or other drug-related problems.

Main Objectives

  • Detect previously unrecognized adverse effects.
  • Monitor known safety problems.
  • Identify risk factors.
  • Improve the safe use of medicines.
  • Reduce preventable drug-related harm.
Key Concept:

Pharmacovigilance helps improve medication safety by continuously monitoring medicines after and during their use.

12. ADR Reporting

Suspected adverse drug reactions should be recognized, documented and reported through appropriate local pharmacovigilance systems.

Important Information to Document

  • Patient information as required by the reporting system.
  • Name of suspected medicine.
  • Dose and route of administration.
  • Time of starting the medicine.
  • Description of the reaction.
  • Time of onset.
  • Other medicines being taken.
  • Relevant medical history.
  • Outcome and treatment of the reaction.

13. Prevention of Adverse Drug Reactions

Many medication-related problems can be reduced through careful prescribing and monitoring.

  • Take a complete medication and allergy history.
  • Use the correct drug, dose, route and duration.
  • Consider renal and hepatic function when appropriate.
  • Check for important drug interactions.
  • Avoid unnecessary polypharmacy.
  • Monitor patients receiving high-risk medicines.
  • Educate patients about important warning symptoms.
  • Review treatment regularly.

14. General Management of ADRs

Management depends on the type and severity of the reaction. The patient should be assessed clinically and appropriate treatment should be provided.

General Principles

  1. Assess the patient's condition and severity of reaction.
  2. Identify the suspected medicine.
  3. Consider whether the medicine should be stopped, reduced or replaced.
  4. Provide supportive and specific treatment when indicated.
  5. Monitor the patient for worsening symptoms.
  6. Document and report significant suspected ADRs.
Clinical Safety:

Severe reactions such as anaphylaxis are medical emergencies and require immediate appropriate emergency treatment.

15. Common Examples of ADRs

Drug / Drug Class Possible Adverse Effect
Insulin Hypoglycemia
Opioids Respiratory depression, constipation, sedation
NSAIDs Gastrointestinal irritation and bleeding
Anticoagulants Bleeding
Antihypertensive drugs Hypotension or dizziness
Some antibiotics Allergic reactions or gastrointestinal effects

16. Type A vs Type B ADRs

Feature Type A Type B
Name Augmented Bizarre
Predictability Usually predictable Usually unpredictable
Dose relationship Usually dose-related Usually not dose-related
Mechanism Related to known pharmacological action Often immune-mediated or idiosyncratic
Frequency More common Less common
Example Hypoglycemia from insulin Anaphylaxis

17. Important Exam Points

  • ADR means an unwanted and harmful response to a medicine at normally used doses.
  • Type A ADRs are predictable and usually dose-related.
  • Type B ADRs are unpredictable and usually not dose-related.
  • Type A = Augmented.
  • Type B = Bizarre.
  • Type C = Chronic.
  • Type D = Delayed.
  • Type E = End of use.
  • Type F = Failure of therapy.
  • Pharmacovigilance focuses on medicine safety and detection/prevention of drug-related problems.
  • Serious and severe are not synonymous.
  • Medication errors are preventable events associated with medication use.

18. Quick Revision

ADR Quick Recall:

Type A → Augmented → Predictable → Dose-related

Type B → Bizarre → Unpredictable → Usually not dose-related

Type C → Chronic → Long-term exposure

Type D → Delayed → Appears later

Type E → End of use → Withdrawal

Type F → Failure → Unexpected treatment failure

19. Adverse Drug Reactions - Important MCQs

Q1. ADR stands for:

A. Active Drug Response
B. Adverse Drug Reaction
C. Advanced Drug Regulation
D. Active Dose Reaction

Answer: B. Adverse Drug Reaction
Q2. Type A adverse drug reactions are usually:

A. Unpredictable
B. Dose-related and predictable
C. Always allergic
D. Always genetic

Answer: B. Dose-related and predictable
Q3. Type B adverse drug reactions are also called:

A. Augmented
B. Bizarre
C. Chronic
D. Delayed

Answer: B. Bizarre
Q4. Which is an example of a Type A reaction?

A. Hypoglycemia caused by insulin
B. Anaphylaxis
C. Idiosyncratic reaction
D. Delayed carcinogenic effect

Answer: A. Hypoglycemia caused by insulin
Q5. Pharmacovigilance primarily deals with:

A. Drug manufacturing only
B. Drug safety and adverse effects
C. Drug packaging only
D. Drug pricing only

Answer: B. Drug safety and adverse effects
Q6. Which Type is associated with withdrawal reactions?

A. Type A
B. Type B
C. Type D
D. Type E

Answer: D. Type E
Q7. Which Type is associated with unexpected failure of therapy?

A. Type A
B. Type C
C. Type E
D. Type F

Answer: D. Type F
Q8. Which statement is correct?

A. Serious and severe always mean exactly the same thing.
B. Type A reactions are usually unpredictable.
C. Type B reactions are usually unpredictable.
D. Pharmacovigilance is only concerned with drug prices.

Answer: C. Type B reactions are usually unpredictable.
