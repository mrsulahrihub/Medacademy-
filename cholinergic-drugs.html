Cholinergic Drugs

MedAcademy - Medical Students

1. Introduction to Cholinergic Drugs

Cholinergic drugs are drugs that mimic, enhance, or modify the actions of acetylcholine (ACh).

Acetylcholine is an important neurotransmitter in the autonomic nervous system, neuromuscular junction, and central nervous system.

High-Yield: Cholinergic drugs are broadly divided into:
  • Direct-acting cholinergic agonists
  • Indirect-acting cholinergic agonists
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors
  • Antimuscarinic drugs

2. Cholinergic Receptors

Acetylcholine acts mainly through two types of receptors:

Receptor Type Main Locations
Muscarinic G-protein coupled receptor Heart, smooth muscle, glands, CNS
Nicotinic Ligand-gated ion channel Autonomic ganglia, adrenal medulla, neuromuscular junction

Muscarinic Receptors

  • M1: Gq → ↑ IP3/DAG
  • M2: Gi → ↓ cAMP → slows heart
  • M3: Gq → ↑ IP3/DAG → gland secretion and smooth muscle contraction

Nicotinic Receptors

  • Nn: Autonomic ganglia and adrenal medulla
  • Nm: Neuromuscular junction
Remember: M1, M3 → Gq M2 → Gi Nicotinic → Ligand-gated ion channel

3. Direct-Acting Cholinergic Agonists

Direct-acting cholinergic agonists bind directly to cholinergic receptors and activate them.

Important Drugs

  • Bethanechol
  • Pilocarpine
  • Carbachol
  • Methacholine

Bethanechol

Bethanechol is primarily a muscarinic agonist.

Uses:
  • Urinary retention
  • Postoperative bladder atony
  • Neurogenic bladder

Pilocarpine

Pilocarpine is a muscarinic agonist that increases glandular secretion.

Uses:
  • Glaucoma
  • Dry mouth (xerostomia)

Methacholine

Methacholine is mainly used as a bronchial challenge agent to help diagnose airway hyperreactivity.

4. Nicotinic Agonists

Nicotinic agonists stimulate nicotinic acetylcholine receptors.

Nicotine

Nicotine stimulates nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia and the CNS. At higher or prolonged exposure, it can produce receptor desensitization and complex autonomic effects.

Nicotine is an important example of a nicotinic receptor agonist.

5. Indirect-Acting Cholinergic Drugs

Indirect-acting cholinergic drugs inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase (AChE).

AChE normally breaks down acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft. Inhibition of AChE increases the concentration and duration of action of acetylcholine.

Mechanism: AChE inhibition → ↑ Acetylcholine → ↑ Muscarinic + Nicotinic effects

6. Reversible Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors

Important Drugs

  • Neostigmine
  • Pyridostigmine
  • Physostigmine
  • Edrophonium
  • Donepezil
  • Rivastigmine
  • Galantamine

Neostigmine

  • Does not significantly cross the blood-brain barrier
  • Increases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction
  • Used in myasthenia gravis
  • Can be used to reverse non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockade

Pyridostigmine

  • Important long-term treatment for myasthenia gravis
  • Primarily acts peripherally

Physostigmine

  • Crosses the blood-brain barrier
  • Can be used in severe central antimuscarinic toxicity

Donepezil

Donepezil is a centrally acting AChE inhibitor used in Alzheimer's disease to improve cholinergic neurotransmission.

7. Irreversible Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors

Organophosphates are important irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitors.

Examples

  • Parathion
  • Malathion
  • Diazinon
  • Sarin
  • Other organophosphate pesticides and nerve agents
Organophosphates cause prolonged inhibition of AChE, leading to excessive acetylcholine accumulation.

8. Clinical Effects of Excess Acetylcholine

Excessive cholinergic stimulation can produce muscarinic and nicotinic effects.

System Effect
Eye Miosis, blurred vision
Glands Salivation, lacrimation, sweating
Lungs Bronchoconstriction, increased bronchial secretions
Heart Bradycardia
GI tract Increased motility, abdominal cramps, diarrhea
Bladder Increased detrusor contraction
Neuromuscular junction Fasciculations, weakness, paralysis in severe toxicity
Classic mnemonic: DUMBBELSS
  • Diarrhea
  • Urination
  • Miosis
  • Bradycardia / Bronchospasm
  • Bronchorrhea
  • Emesis
  • Lacrimation
  • Salivation
  • Sweating

9. Cholinergic Crisis

A cholinergic crisis occurs when there is excessive cholinergic stimulation, which may occur with excessive cholinesterase inhibition.

Features

  • Marked salivation
  • Lacrimation
  • Diarrhea
  • Vomiting
  • Bradycardia
  • Bronchospasm
  • Bronchorrhea
  • Muscle fasciculations
  • Muscle weakness
  • Respiratory failure in severe cases
Severe cholinergic toxicity can be life-threatening because of bronchorrhea, bronchospasm, muscle weakness, and respiratory failure.

10. Organophosphate Poisoning

Organophosphate poisoning produces excessive acetylcholine because acetylcholinesterase is inhibited.

Clinical Features

  • Salivation
  • Lacrimation
  • Urination
  • Diarrhea
  • Vomiting
  • Miosis
  • Bradycardia
  • Bronchorrhea
  • Bronchospasm
  • Fasciculations
  • Muscle weakness
  • Confusion or seizures in severe poisoning
High-Yield: The combination of excessive secretions, miosis, bronchospasm, and muscle fasciculations should raise suspicion for cholinergic toxicity.

11. Atropine

Atropine is a competitive muscarinic receptor antagonist.

It blocks the effects of acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors.

Important Uses

  • Symptomatic bradycardia in appropriate clinical settings
  • Reduction of excessive secretions in selected situations
  • Organophosphate poisoning
  • Preoperative reduction of secretions in selected cases
Important: In organophosphate poisoning, atropine treats the muscarinic manifestations of toxicity. It does not directly reverse the nicotinic muscle weakness.

12. Pralidoxime (2-PAM)

Pralidoxime is an oxime that can reactivate acetylcholinesterase in organophosphate poisoning if administered before the enzyme undergoes the process known as aging.

Main Action

  • Reactivates inhibited AChE when possible
  • Can improve nicotinic manifestations such as muscle weakness
  • Does not replace atropine
Organophosphate treatment concept: Atropine → treats muscarinic toxicity Pralidoxime → reactivates AChE and helps with nicotinic toxicity Supportive care → airway, breathing, circulation and other critical care

13. Management of Organophosphate Poisoning

  1. Ensure scene and healthcare-worker safety.
  2. Assess and support airway, breathing, and circulation.
  3. Remove contaminated clothing and perform appropriate decontamination.
  4. Give atropine and titrate to improvement in clinically important muscarinic effects.
  5. Give pralidoxime when indicated, particularly for significant organophosphate poisoning.
  6. Treat seizures with appropriate therapy when present.
  7. Continue monitoring and supportive care.
Exam Point: Atropine + pralidoxime are the classic antidotal drugs for significant organophosphate poisoning, along with essential supportive care.

14. Adverse Effects of Cholinergic Drugs

  • Salivation
  • Sweating
  • Lacrimation
  • Abdominal cramps
  • Diarrhea
  • Urination
  • Bronchospasm
  • Bradycardia
  • Miosis
  • Muscle fasciculations
  • Muscle weakness
Excessive cholinergic stimulation produces effects opposite to many antimuscarinic effects.

15. Important Cautions / Contraindications

Cholinergic agonists may be inappropriate or require caution in conditions where increased cholinergic activity could worsen the patient's condition.

  • Asthma or significant bronchospasm
  • Peptic ulcer disease
  • Bradycardia or conduction abnormalities
  • Mechanical gastrointestinal obstruction
  • Mechanical urinary obstruction

The exact contraindications and precautions depend on the individual drug and clinical situation.

17. Quick Revision

  • Acetylcholine acts on muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.
  • M1 and M3 are Gq-coupled.
  • M2 is Gi-coupled.
  • Nicotinic receptors are ligand-gated ion channels.
  • Bethanechol is useful for urinary retention.
  • Pilocarpine is useful for glaucoma and xerostomia.
  • Neostigmine and pyridostigmine are important in myasthenia gravis.
  • Physostigmine crosses the blood-brain barrier.
  • Donepezil is used in Alzheimer's disease.
  • Organophosphates inhibit AChE for prolonged periods.
  • Atropine blocks muscarinic receptors.
  • Pralidoxime can reactivate AChE before aging in organophosphate poisoning.
  • Severe cholinergic toxicity can cause respiratory failure.

18. MCQs

Q1. Which receptor is Gi-coupled?

  1. M1
  2. M2
  3. M3
  4. Nicotinic

Answer: B — M2

Q2. Which drug is useful for urinary retention?

  1. Atropine
  2. Bethanechol
  3. Propranolol
  4. Scopolamine

Answer: B — Bethanechol

Q3. Which drug is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor?

  1. Neostigmine
  2. Atropine
  3. Prazosin
  4. Salbutamol

Answer: A — Neostigmine

Q4. Which AChE inhibitor crosses the blood-brain barrier?

  1. Neostigmine
  2. Pyridostigmine
  3. Physostigmine
  4. Glycopyrrolate

Answer: C — Physostigmine

Q5. Which drug is commonly used for Alzheimer's disease?

  1. Donepezil
  2. Bethanechol
  3. Atropine
  4. Pralidoxime

Answer: A — Donepezil

Q6. Organophosphates primarily cause toxicity by:

  1. Blocking dopamine receptors
  2. Blocking muscarinic receptors
  3. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase
  4. Blocking beta receptors

Answer: C — Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase

Q7. Which drug blocks muscarinic receptors?

  1. Atropine
  2. Pilocarpine
  3. Bethanechol
  4. Neostigmine

Answer: A — Atropine

Q8. Which drug can reactivate acetylcholinesterase in organophosphate poisoning before aging?

  1. Atropine
  2. Pralidoxime
  3. Pilocarpine
  4. Donepezil

Answer: B — Pralidoxime

Q9. Which is a muscarinic receptor agonist?

  1. Pilocarpine
  2. Atropine
  3. Propranolol
  4. Phenylephrine

Answer: A — Pilocarpine

Q10. Severe cholinergic toxicity may cause:

  1. Bronchorrhea and respiratory failure
  2. Severe mydriasis only
  3. Urinary retention
  4. Dry skin

Answer: A — Bronchorrhea and respiratory failure
