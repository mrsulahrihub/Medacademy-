1. Introduction to Clinical Pharmacology

Clinical pharmacology is the study of medicines in humans, including their safe and effective use, therapeutic benefits, adverse effects, interactions, individual variation and monitoring.

Four Major Topics:
  • Prescription Writing
  • Rational Drug Use
  • Drug Safety
  • Therapeutic Drug Monitoring

The ultimate aim is to select the right treatment for the right patient, using an appropriate medicine, dose, route and duration while minimizing avoidable harm.

3. Prescription Writing

A prescription is a written or electronic instruction from an authorized prescriber for the treatment of a patient.

Essential Components of a Prescription

  1. Patient name and appropriate identifying information.
  2. Date of prescription.
  3. Generic name of the medicine whenever appropriate.
  4. Strength and dosage form.
  5. Dose to be administered.
  6. Route of administration.
  7. Frequency or dosing interval.
  8. Duration or quantity to be supplied.
  9. Specific instructions for the patient.
  10. Prescriber identification and signature/authentication as required.

Common Prescription Format

Component Example/Explanation
Patient Name, age and relevant clinical information
Drug Preferably generic name
Strength e.g. 500 mg
Dosage form Tablet, capsule, syrup, injection etc.
Dose Amount administered at each dose
Route Oral, IV, IM, topical etc.
Frequency How often the medicine is given
Duration How long treatment should continue
Patient instructions Important administration and precautionary information

Example of a Teaching Prescription

Patient: Adult patient
Drug: Paracetamol
Strength: 500 mg tablet
Route: Oral
Directions: Take according to the prescribed dosing schedule for the specified indication and duration.

A real prescription must be individualized according to the patient's diagnosis, age, weight when relevant, organ function, allergies, interactions and applicable local prescribing requirements.

Common Prescription Errors

  • Illegible or ambiguous writing.
  • Wrong patient.
  • Wrong drug or strength.
  • Wrong dose or frequency.
  • Missing route of administration.
  • Unclear duration.
  • Unsafe abbreviations.
  • Failure to check allergies or interactions.
  • Failure to consider renal or hepatic impairment.
  • Unnecessary polypharmacy.
MBBS Point: Always check the patient, indication, drug, dose, route, frequency, duration, allergies, contraindications and important interactions before finalizing a prescription.

4. Rational Drug Use

Rational use of medicines means that patients receive medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses appropriate to their individual requirements, for an adequate period and at the lowest appropriate cost.

Steps of Rational Prescribing

  1. Define the patient's problem.
  2. Specify the therapeutic objective.
  3. Consider non-drug treatment when appropriate.
  4. Select a suitable drug.
  5. Determine the correct dosage regimen.
  6. Write the prescription clearly.
  7. Give the patient appropriate information.
  8. Monitor the response.
  9. Modify or stop treatment when indicated.

Factors to Consider Before Choosing a Drug

  • Diagnosis and therapeutic goal.
  • Age and body weight when relevant.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding status when relevant.
  • Renal function.
  • Hepatic function.
  • Drug allergies.
  • Contraindications.
  • Drug-drug and drug-food interactions.
  • Cost and availability.
  • Patient adherence.

Irrational Drug Use

Examples include unnecessary polypharmacy, inappropriate antibiotic use, incorrect dosing, unnecessary injections, failure to follow evidence-based guidelines and inappropriate self-medication.

Advantages of Rational Drug Use

  • Improves therapeutic outcomes.
  • Reduces adverse drug reactions.
  • Reduces medication errors.
  • Helps prevent antimicrobial resistance.
  • Reduces unnecessary healthcare expenditure.
  • Improves patient adherence.

5. Drug Safety

Drug safety focuses on identifying, preventing and managing medication-related harm throughout the medicine-use process.

Important Areas of Drug Safety

  • Correct prescribing.
  • Correct dispensing.
  • Correct administration.
  • Monitoring therapeutic response.
  • Recognition of adverse drug reactions.
  • Detection of drug interactions.
  • Medication-error reporting.
  • Pharmacovigilance.

Pharmacovigilance

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities related to detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects and other drug-related problems.

Medication Errors

Error Example
Prescribing error Incorrect dose or inappropriate medicine
Dispensing error Wrong medicine supplied
Administration error Wrong route or wrong patient
Monitoring error Failure to monitor an important adverse effect

Five Rights of Medication Administration

  • Right patient.
  • Right drug.
  • Right dose.
  • Right route.
  • Right time.

These checks are an important medication-safety principle; safe systems also emphasize clear communication, avoiding error-prone abbreviations and reporting medication incidents.

Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction is a harmful and unintended response associated with the use of a medicine at normally used doses. Important steps include recognizing the reaction, assessing severity, managing the patient and reporting suspected reactions through appropriate systems.

Drug Safety Rule: Never assume that a medicine is completely risk-free. The prescriber must balance expected benefit against potential harm.

6. Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM)

Therapeutic Drug Monitoring involves measuring drug concentrations in biological samples and using the results, together with clinical information, to optimize therapy.

Why is TDM Used?

  • To maintain drug concentration within an appropriate therapeutic range.
  • To reduce toxicity.
  • To investigate suspected treatment failure.
  • To assess adherence in selected situations.
  • To individualize dosing when pharmacokinetic variability is important.

Drugs Commonly Associated with TDM

Drug Why Monitoring May Be Useful
Digoxin Narrow therapeutic range; toxicity can be serious
Lithium Narrow therapeutic range and important renal/electrolyte effects
Phenytoin Variable pharmacokinetics and concentration-dependent toxicity
Valproate Concentration monitoring can be useful in selected clinical situations
Carbamazepine Monitoring may assist in selected cases
Phenobarbital Concentration monitoring can be useful
Some aminoglycosides Monitoring can help optimize exposure and reduce toxicity
Vancomycin Exposure-guided monitoring is used in selected serious infections

Important Factors Affecting Drug Concentration

  • Dose.
  • Absorption.
  • Distribution.
  • Metabolism.
  • Renal elimination.
  • Liver function.
  • Age.
  • Drug interactions.
  • Genetic variability.
  • Adherence.

Peak and Trough Concentrations

The timing of blood sampling is critical. A concentration should be interpreted in relation to when the last dose was given and the pharmacokinetic properties of the drug.

  • Trough: concentration immediately before the next dose.
  • Peak: concentration measured at an appropriate time after dosing when a peak measurement is clinically relevant.
High-Yield Point: A drug level should never be interpreted in isolation. The patient's clinical condition, dose, sampling time, organ function and laboratory method must also be considered.

7. Important TDM Examples

Lithium

  • Used as a mood stabilizer.
  • Has a narrow therapeutic range.
  • Renally eliminated.
  • Renal function and thyroid function are important to monitor.
  • Toxicity may cause tremor, gastrointestinal symptoms, confusion and neurological abnormalities.
  • Changes in sodium/fluid balance and several medicines can alter lithium concentrations.

Digoxin

  • Has a narrow therapeutic range.
  • Renal impairment can increase exposure.
  • Hypokalemia increases risk of toxicity.
  • Toxicity may produce gastrointestinal, neurological and cardiac manifestations.

Phenytoin

  • Shows nonlinear pharmacokinetics at therapeutic concentrations.
  • Highly protein bound.
  • Low albumin can affect interpretation of total concentration.
  • Drug interactions can significantly alter concentration.

Aminoglycosides

  • Examples include gentamicin and amikacin.
  • Renal function is important.
  • Excess exposure may cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity.
  • Drug-level monitoring may be used depending on regimen and clinical setting.

Topic Main Purpose
Prescription Writing Communicate a clear and complete treatment order
Rational Drug Use Select the most appropriate treatment for the patient

9. MBBS-Level Questions & Answers

Q1. Define clinical pharmacology.

Answer: Clinical pharmacology is the study of medicines and their effects in humans, including therapeutic use, adverse effects, interactions and safe individualized treatment.

Q2. What are the essential components of a prescription?

Answer: Patient identification, date, drug name, strength, dosage form, dose, route, frequency, duration/quantity, instructions and prescriber authentication.

Q3. What is rational drug use?

Answer: It means selecting medicines appropriate to the patient's clinical needs, using an appropriate dose and duration while considering safety, effectiveness, patient factors and cost.

Q4. What are the steps of rational prescribing?

Answer: Define the problem, specify the therapeutic objective, consider treatment options, select the drug, determine dosage, write the prescription, give instructions and monitor the response.

Q5. What is pharmacovigilance?

Answer: Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities concerned with detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects and other drug-related problems.

Q6. What is a medication error?

Answer: A preventable error that may occur during prescribing, dispensing, administration or monitoring of medicines and may lead to inappropriate medicine use or patient harm.

Q7. What are the five rights of medication administration?

Answer: Right patient, right drug, right dose, right route and right time.

Q8. Define therapeutic drug monitoring.

Answer: TDM is the measurement and interpretation of drug concentrations, together with clinical information, to optimize treatment for selected medicines.

Q9. Why is TDM important?

Answer: It is useful when there is substantial pharmacokinetic variability, a narrow therapeutic range, a clear concentration-effect/toxicity relationship, or when concentration measurement can meaningfully guide dosing.

Q10. Give examples of drugs commonly monitored by TDM.

Answer: Lithium, digoxin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, valproate, selected aminoglycosides and vancomycin.

Q11. Why can phenytoin concentration be difficult to interpret?

Answer: Phenytoin is highly protein bound and has nonlinear pharmacokinetics. Low albumin and interacting medicines can affect the relationship between measured total concentration and active drug exposure.

Q12. Why is lithium monitoring important?

Answer: Lithium has a narrow therapeutic range and is mainly eliminated by the kidneys. Changes in renal function, fluid balance, sodium balance and interacting drugs can alter lithium concentrations and toxicity risk.

Q13. What is rational prescribing important for antibiotics?

Answer: It helps ensure that antibiotics are used only when indicated and that the appropriate agent, dose and duration are selected, reducing unnecessary exposure and helping limit antimicrobial resistance.

Q14. What is the difference between an adverse drug reaction and a medication error?

Answer: An adverse drug reaction is a harmful unintended response associated with a medicine at normal doses. A medication error is a preventable mistake in the medicine-use process.

Q15. Why should generic drug names be preferred in prescribing?

Answer: Generic names clearly identify the active pharmaceutical ingredient and reduce confusion between different brand names containing the same medicine.
