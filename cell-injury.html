1. Introduction to Cell Injury

Cell injury occurs when a cell is exposed to stress or a harmful stimulus that exceeds its ability to adapt. The outcome depends on the type, duration and severity of the injury as well as the type and metabolic state of the cell.

Normal Cell
→
Cellular Adaptation
→
Reversible Injury
→
Irreversible Injury
→
Cell Death

Mild or transient injury may be reversible if the damaging stimulus is removed. Persistent or severe injury can become irreversible and lead to cell death.

3. Causes of Cell Injury

1. Oxygen Deprivation

  • Hypoxia.
  • Ischemia.
  • Respiratory failure.
  • Anemia.
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning.

2. Physical Agents

  • Trauma.
  • Extreme temperatures.
  • Radiation.
  • Electric shock.
  • Changes in atmospheric pressure.

3. Chemical Agents

  • Drugs and therapeutic agents.
  • Industrial chemicals.
  • Poisons.
  • Heavy metals.
  • Environmental pollutants.

4. Infectious Agents

  • Bacteria.
  • Viruses.
  • Fungi.
  • Protozoa.
  • Parasites.

5. Immunologic Reactions

  • Autoimmune diseases.
  • Hypersensitivity reactions.
  • Excessive inflammatory responses.

6. Genetic Abnormalities

  • Abnormal proteins.
  • Enzyme defects.
  • DNA abnormalities.
  • Chromosomal abnormalities.

7. Nutritional Imbalances

  • Protein-calorie deficiency.
  • Vitamin deficiency.
  • Obesity.
  • Excessive nutrient intake.

8. Aging

Cellular aging reduces the ability of cells to repair damage and maintain normal homeostasis.

4. Hypoxia and Ischemia

Hypoxia

Hypoxia means inadequate oxygen availability to cells.

Ischemia

Ischemia is reduced blood flow to tissue, causing reduced oxygen and nutrient delivery and impaired removal of metabolic waste.

Important: Ischemia is generally more harmful than hypoxia alone because it causes both oxygen deprivation and reduced delivery of nutrients with impaired removal of metabolic waste.

Common Causes of Hypoxia

  • Respiratory failure.
  • Anemia.
  • Carbon monoxide poisoning.
  • Reduced blood flow.

Common Causes of Ischemia

  • Thrombosis.
  • Embolism.
  • Atherosclerosis.
  • Vascular obstruction.

5. Reversible Cell Injury

Reversible injury occurs when the cell can recover after removal of the injurious stimulus.

Major Morphologic Features

  • Cellular swelling.
  • Hydropic change.
  • Fatty change.
  • Plasma membrane blebbing.
  • Loss or blunting of microvilli.
  • Mitochondrial swelling.
  • Endoplasmic reticulum dilation.
  • Detachment of ribosomes.
  • Chromatin clumping.
  • Reduced protein synthesis.

Cellular Swelling

Cellular swelling is one of the earliest manifestations of reversible injury. It commonly results from failure of energy-dependent ion pumps, particularly the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, causing intracellular sodium and water accumulation.

Fatty Change

Fatty change refers to abnormal accumulation of lipid droplets in the cytoplasm. It is especially important in organs involved in lipid metabolism, such as the liver.

6. Irreversible Cell Injury

If the injury becomes sufficiently severe or prolonged, the cell reaches a point from which recovery is no longer possible and eventually dies.

Major Features

  • Severe mitochondrial dysfunction.
  • Failure of oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Severe ATP depletion.
  • Extensive plasma membrane damage.
  • Lysosomal membrane damage.
  • Massive calcium influx.
  • Leakage of intracellular enzymes.
  • Nuclear destruction.
Two major features of irreversibility:

1. Inability to reverse mitochondrial dysfunction and restore oxidative phosphorylation.
2. Severe membrane damage.

7. ATP Depletion

ATP is essential for many cellular functions. Depletion of ATP is a major mechanism of cell injury, particularly in hypoxic and ischemic injury.

Consequences of ATP Depletion

  1. Failure of Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase.
  2. Intracellular sodium accumulation.
  3. Water enters the cell.
  4. Cellular swelling.
  5. Increased anaerobic glycolysis.
  6. Increased lactic acid production.
  7. Decreased intracellular pH.
  8. Chromatin clumping.
  9. Failure of protein synthesis.
  10. Progression to irreversible injury if severe.
Exam Chain: ATP ↓ → Na⁺/K⁺ pump failure → Na⁺ + water enter cell → cellular swelling.

8. Mitochondrial Damage

Mitochondria are central to cellular energy production. Severe mitochondrial damage can cause ATP depletion and may also contribute to apoptosis through release of pro-apoptotic proteins.

Effects

  • Reduced ATP production.
  • Loss of oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Increased reactive oxygen species.
  • Release of proteins that can activate apoptosis.
  • Progression toward cell death.

9. Calcium Influx

Injured cells may develop abnormal increases in intracellular calcium. Excess calcium activates enzymes that damage cellular components.

Calcium Activates

  • Phospholipases → membrane damage.
  • Proteases → cytoskeletal and membrane protein damage.
  • Endonucleases → DNA damage.
  • ATPases → further ATP depletion.
High-Yield: ↑ Intracellular Ca²⁺ → activation of destructive enzymes → membrane, protein and DNA damage.

10. Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Reactive oxygen species are highly reactive molecules that can damage lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.

Important ROS

  • Superoxide (O₂•⁻).
  • Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂).
  • Hydroxyl radical (OH•).

Mechanisms of Damage

  • Lipid peroxidation of membranes.
  • Protein oxidation and modification.
  • DNA damage.
  • Mitochondrial damage.

Antioxidant Defenses

  • Superoxide dismutase.
  • Catalase.
  • Glutathione peroxidase.
  • Vitamins and other antioxidant systems.

11. Membrane Damage

Membrane integrity is essential for cell survival. Damage to the plasma, mitochondrial or lysosomal membranes can contribute to irreversible injury.

Consequences of Plasma Membrane Damage

  • Loss of cellular contents.
  • Abnormal ion influx.
  • Loss of homeostasis.
  • Enzyme leakage.

Mitochondrial Membrane Damage

  • ATP production decreases.
  • Pro-apoptotic proteins may be released.

Lysosomal Membrane Damage

  • Hydrolytic enzymes are released.
  • Cellular components are digested.

12. Nuclear Changes in Cell Death

13. Necrosis

Necrosis is a form of cell death associated with loss of membrane integrity, enzymatic digestion of cellular components and an inflammatory response.

Features of Necrosis

  • Cell swelling.
  • Membrane disruption.
  • Leakage of cellular contents.
  • Nuclear changes.
  • Inflammation.

Major Types of Necrosis

Type Typical Examples
Coagulative Ischemic infarction of most solid organs except brain
Liquefactive Brain infarction and many bacterial/fungal infections
Caseous Tuberculosis
Fat Acute pancreatitis and traumatic fat necrosis
Fibrinoid Immune-mediated vascular injury
Gangrenous Clinical pattern usually involving ischemic tissue; may be dry or complicated by infection

14. Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death in which individual cells activate intracellular death pathways. It is important in normal physiology and in several pathological conditions.

Physiological Examples

  • Embryonic development.
  • Removal of cells during tissue remodeling.
  • Elimination of cells after hormone withdrawal.
  • Removal of some immune cells after completion of an immune response.

Pathological Causes

  • DNA damage.
  • Accumulation of misfolded proteins.
  • Some viral infections.
  • Cell death following certain pathological stresses.

Morphologic Features

  • Cell shrinkage.
  • Chromatin condensation.
  • Nuclear fragmentation.
  • Formation of apoptotic bodies.
  • Usually no significant surrounding inflammation.

Major Pathways

Pathway Main Trigger
Intrinsic pathway Mitochondrial stress, DNA damage, growth-factor withdrawal
Extrinsic pathway Death receptors such as Fas and TNF-receptor family signals

Caspases

Caspases are proteolytic enzymes that execute apoptosis. Initiator caspases activate downstream executioner caspases, resulting in controlled destruction of the cell.

15. Necrosis vs Apoptosis

Feature Necrosis Apoptosis
Cell size Usually swelling Usually shrinkage
Cell membrane Disrupted Generally maintained until apoptotic bodies form
Nuclear changes Pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis Chromatin condensation and fragmentation
Cell contents Leak into extracellular space Packaged into apoptotic bodies
Inflammation Usually present Usually absent/minimal
Typical pattern Often groups of cells Often individual cells
Role Usually pathologic Physiologic and pathologic

16. Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury

Reperfusion can sometimes cause additional injury to previously ischemic tissue. Important mechanisms include oxidative stress, inflammation and calcium-related injury.

Important Mechanisms

  • Generation of reactive oxygen species.
  • Calcium overload.
  • Inflammatory cell activation.
  • Mitochondrial dysfunction.
  • Complement-related injury.
Concept: Restoration of blood flow is essential, but reperfusion of severely ischemic tissue can paradoxically contribute to additional cellular damage.

18. MBBS-Level Questions & Answers

Q1. Define cell injury.

Answer: Cell injury is a state in which a cell is unable to maintain normal structure and function because the stress or harmful stimulus exceeds its adaptive capacity.

Q2. What determines the outcome of cell injury?

Answer: The type, severity and duration of the stimulus, along with the type, metabolic state and adaptability of the cell.

Q3. What are the two main types of cell injury?

Answer: Reversible cell injury and irreversible cell injury.

Q4. What is the most common morphologic manifestation of reversible injury?

Answer: Cellular swelling.

Q5. What causes cellular swelling?

Answer: Failure of ATP-dependent ion pumps, especially the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase, leading to intracellular sodium and water accumulation.

Q6. What are the major mechanisms of cell injury?

Answer: ATP depletion, mitochondrial damage, calcium influx, accumulation of ROS, membrane damage, DNA damage and accumulation of misfolded proteins.

Q7. What are the two important features of irreversible injury?

Answer: Severe mitochondrial dysfunction with failure to restore oxidative phosphorylation and profound membrane damage.

Q8. Define necrosis.

Answer: Necrosis is cell death characterized by loss of membrane integrity, enzymatic digestion of cellular components and an associated inflammatory response.

Q9. Define apoptosis.

Answer: Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death in which individual cells undergo controlled destruction, usually without significant surrounding inflammation.

Q10. What are the nuclear changes in necrosis?

Answer: Pyknosis, karyorrhexis and karyolysis.

Q11. What is pyknosis?

Answer: Pyknosis is nuclear shrinkage with chromatin condensation.

Q12. What is karyorrhexis?

Answer: Karyorrhexis is fragmentation of the nucleus.

Q13. What is karyolysis?

Answer: Karyolysis is dissolution and fading of the nucleus.

Q14. Give examples of coagulative necrosis.

Answer: Ischemic infarction of most solid organs such as the heart and kidney, with the major exception of the brain.

Q15. Which type of necrosis occurs in tuberculosis?

Answer: Caseous necrosis.

Q16. Which type of necrosis occurs in brain infarction?

Answer: Liquefactive necrosis.

Q17. What is the major difference between necrosis and apoptosis?

Answer: Necrosis usually involves membrane disruption and inflammation, whereas apoptosis is a regulated process with cell shrinkage and minimal inflammation.

Q18. What is the role of ROS in cell injury?

Answer: ROS damage lipids, proteins and DNA through oxidative reactions, contributing to membrane damage and cellular dysfunction.

Q19. What happens when intracellular calcium increases excessively?

Answer: Calcium activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases and ATPases, causing membrane, protein, DNA and energy damage.

Q20. What is ischemia-reperfusion injury?

Answer: It is additional cellular injury that can occur when blood flow is restored to previously ischemic tissue, partly due to ROS generation, calcium overload and inflammatory mechanisms.
