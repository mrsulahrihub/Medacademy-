Introduction to Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics is the study of what the body does to a drug after administration. It describes how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized and eliminated.

Key Concept: Pharmacokinetics is commonly summarized as ADME: Absorption → Distribution → Metabolism → Excretion.

1. Absorption

Absorption is the process by which a drug moves from its site of administration into the systemic circulation.

Factors Affecting Absorption

  • Route of administration
  • Blood flow to the absorption site
  • Surface area
  • Drug formulation
  • Gastrointestinal motility
  • pH of the environment
  • Food and other drugs

Bioavailability

Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation in unchanged form.

Important: Intravenous administration generally has a bioavailability of 100%.

2. Distribution

Distribution is the reversible movement of a drug from the bloodstream into body tissues and fluids.

Factors Affecting Distribution

  • Blood flow to tissues
  • Capillary permeability
  • Plasma protein binding
  • Tissue binding
  • Lipid solubility
  • Body composition

Plasma Protein Binding

Many drugs bind reversibly to plasma proteins such as albumin. Only the unbound fraction is generally available to leave the bloodstream and produce pharmacological effects.

3. Metabolism

Drug metabolism is the chemical modification of drugs by enzymes in the body. The liver is the major organ responsible for drug metabolism.

Phase I Reactions

  • Oxidation
  • Reduction
  • Hydrolysis

Phase II Reactions

  • Glucuronidation
  • Sulfation
  • Acetylation
  • Methylation
  • Conjugation with glutathione
Purpose of metabolism: Drug metabolism often makes substances more water-soluble and easier to eliminate, although metabolism can sometimes produce active or toxic metabolites.

4. First-Pass Effect

The first-pass effect occurs when a drug administered by certain routes, particularly the oral route, is metabolized in the intestinal wall and liver before reaching the systemic circulation.

This can reduce the amount of unchanged drug reaching the systemic circulation.

5. Excretion

Excretion is the removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body.

Main Routes of Excretion

  • Kidneys → urine
  • Bile → feces
  • Lungs → exhaled air
  • Breast milk
  • Sweat and saliva
Major route: The kidneys are the major organs responsible for the elimination of many drugs and their metabolites.

6. Half-Life

The elimination half-life is the time required for the concentration of a drug in the body or plasma to decrease by approximately 50% during the elimination phase.

Clinical Importance

  • Helps determine dosing intervals.
  • Helps estimate the time required to reach steady state.
  • Helps estimate how long a drug may remain in the body.

7. Clearance

Clearance describes the volume of plasma from which a drug is completely removed per unit of time.

Clearance is an important concept for determining drug maintenance dosing.

8. Volume of Distribution

Volume of distribution is a theoretical volume that relates the amount of drug in the body to the concentration of drug in plasma.

High volume of distribution: Generally suggests extensive distribution of the drug into tissues.

9. Steady-State Concentration

Steady state is reached when the rate of drug administration is approximately equal to the rate of drug elimination.

For many drugs, steady state is approached after several elimination half-lives.

⭐ Important Exam Points

  • ADME stands for Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism and Excretion.
  • Intravenous administration generally provides 100% bioavailability.
  • The liver is the major site of drug metabolism.
  • The kidneys are the major organs involved in drug excretion.
  • Only the unbound fraction of many drugs is readily available for distribution and pharmacological action.
  • Half-life is important when determining dosing intervals.
  • First-pass metabolism can reduce the systemic availability of orally administered drugs.
