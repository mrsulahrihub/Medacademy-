💪 Upper Limb Anatomy

Complete MBBS-level high-yield notes, clinical anatomy, MCQs and SEQs.

First Professional MBBS • Anatomy

📖 1. Introduction

The upper limb is a highly mobile part of the human body. It is attached to the axial skeleton mainly through the clavicle at the sternoclavicular joint.

The upper limb is divided into the following major regions:

  1. Pectoral region
  2. Shoulder region
  3. Axilla
  4. Arm
  5. Cubital fossa
  6. Forearm
  7. Wrist
  8. Hand
MBBS High-Yield:

The upper limb is designed mainly for positioning the hand in space, grasping objects and performing precise movements.

🦴 2. Bones of Upper Limb

Upper limb bones: clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges.

Major Bones

  • Clavicle
  • Scapula
  • Humerus
  • Radius
  • Ulna
  • 8 carpal bones
  • 5 metacarpals
  • 14 phalanges

Important Osteological Landmarks

Bone Important Landmark Clinical Importance
Clavicle Conoid tubercle, trapezoid line, sternal and acromial ends Commonly fractured bone
Scapula Spine, acromion, coracoid process, glenoid cavity Winging of scapula may occur with long thoracic nerve injury
Humerus Surgical neck, radial groove, medial and lateral epicondyles Important sites of nerve injury
Radius Head, neck, radial tuberosity, styloid process Colles fracture and radial head injuries
Ulna Olecranon, coronoid process, trochlear notch Forms major part of elbow articulation
Clinical Correlation:

Fracture of the surgical neck of humerus may damage the axillary nerve and posterior circumflex humeral vessels.

🫁 3. Pectoral Region

The pectoral region contains muscles connecting the upper limb with the thoracic wall.

Important Muscles

  • Pectoralis major
  • Pectoralis minor
  • Subclavius
  • Serratus anterior

Pectoralis Major

  • Large fan-shaped muscle of anterior chest wall.
  • Acts mainly on the shoulder joint.
  • Adducts and medially rotates the arm.
  • Clavicular head helps flex the arm.
  • Innervated by medial and lateral pectoral nerves.

Serratus Anterior

  • Arises from upper ribs.
  • Inserts on medial border of scapula.
  • Protracts the scapula.
  • Helps in upward rotation of scapula.
  • Supplied by long thoracic nerve.
Important Clinical Point:

Long thoracic nerve injury causes weakness of serratus anterior and may produce winging of scapula.

🩸 4. Axilla

The axilla is a pyramidal space between the upper limb and thoracic wall. It provides a passage for major nerves, vessels and lymphatics.

Boundaries of Axilla

Boundary Structures
Anterior wall Pectoralis major, pectoralis minor and clavipectoral fascia
Posterior wall Subscapularis, teres major and latissimus dorsi
Medial wall Thoracic wall and serratus anterior
Lateral wall Intertubercular sulcus of humerus
Apex Clavicle, first rib and superior border of scapula
Base Skin and axillary fascia

Contents of Axilla

  • Axillary artery and branches
  • Axillary vein
  • Cords and branches of brachial plexus
  • Axillary lymph nodes
  • Fat and connective tissue
Exam Favourite:

Know the boundaries and contents of axilla. Axilla and brachial plexus are repeatedly tested in MBBS anatomy examinations.

🩸 5. Axillary Artery

The axillary artery is the continuation of the subclavian artery. It becomes the brachial artery at the inferior border of teres major.

Three Parts

Part Location Branches
1st part Between lateral border of first rib and medial border of pectoralis minor Superior thoracic artery
2nd part Behind pectoralis minor Thoracoacromial and lateral thoracic arteries
3rd part From lateral border of pectoralis minor to inferior border of teres major Subscapular, anterior circumflex humeral and posterior circumflex humeral arteries
Mnemonic:

1 - 2 - 3 parts of axillary artery have 1 - 2 - 3 major branches respectively.

🧠 6. Brachial Plexus

Anterior view of the brachial plexus showing roots, trunks, divisions, cords and terminal branches.

The brachial plexus supplies the upper limb. It is formed mainly by the anterior rami of spinal nerves C5-T1.

Organization

Roots → Trunks → Divisions → Cords → Branches

Roots

C5, C6, C7, C8 and T1

Trunks

  • Upper trunk = C5 + C6
  • Middle trunk = C7
  • Lower trunk = C8 + T1

Divisions

  • Each trunk divides into anterior and posterior divisions.

Cords

  • Lateral cord
  • Medial cord
  • Posterior cord

Major Terminal Branches

Nerve Roots Main Motor Association
Musculocutaneous C5-C7 Anterior compartment of arm
Axillary C5-C6 Deltoid and teres minor
Radial C5-T1 Extensors of arm and forearm
Median C5-T1 Most anterior forearm muscles and thenar muscles
Ulnar C8-T1 Many intrinsic muscles of hand

🚑 7. Clinical Anatomy of Brachial Plexus

Erb-Duchenne Palsy

Usually associated with an upper trunk lesion involving C5-C6.

Typical posture is classically described as the "waiter's tip" position.

Klumpke Palsy

Lower trunk lesion involving mainly C8-T1 can produce weakness of intrinsic hand muscles and may result in a claw-hand posture.

Long Thoracic Nerve Injury

Produces weakness of serratus anterior and winging of scapula.

MBBS Clinical Question:

A patient develops winging of the scapula after an injury. Which nerve is most likely damaged?

Answer: Long thoracic nerve.

🦴 8. Shoulder Joint

The shoulder joint is a synovial ball-and-socket joint between the head of humerus and glenoid cavity of scapula.

Movements

  • Flexion
  • Extension
  • Abduction
  • Adduction
  • Medial rotation
  • Lateral rotation
  • Circumduction

Rotator Cuff Muscles

SITS

  • Supraspinatus
  • Infraspinatus
  • Teres minor
  • Subscapularis
Important:

Supraspinatus initiates the first approximately 15° of abduction. Deltoid is the major abductor after the initial phase.

💪 9. Arm Compartments

Anterior Compartment

  • Biceps brachii
  • Brachialis
  • Coracobrachialis

The musculocutaneous nerve is the major nerve of the anterior compartment.

Posterior Compartment

  • Triceps brachii
  • Anconeus

The radial nerve supplies the major muscles of the posterior arm.

Muscle Main Action Nerve
Biceps brachii Elbow flexion and supination Musculocutaneous
Brachialis Powerful elbow flexion Musculocutaneous mainly
Coracobrachialis Flexion and adduction of arm Musculocutaneous
Triceps brachii Elbow extension Radial

🩺 10. Cubital Fossa

Cubital fossa is a triangular depression on the anterior aspect of the elbow.

Boundaries

  • Lateral: brachioradialis
  • Medial: pronator teres
  • Superior: imaginary line between epicondyles

Important Contents

From lateral to medial:

Radial nerve → Biceps tendon → Brachial artery → Median nerve

Clinical Importance:

The median cubital vein lies superficially in the cubital region and is commonly used for venipuncture.

💪 11. Forearm

Anterior Compartment

Mainly responsible for flexion and pronation.

Posterior Compartment

Mainly responsible for extension and supination.

Important Nerves

  • Median nerve
  • Anterior interosseous nerve
  • Ulnar nerve
  • Radial nerve and its deep branch

Important Clinical Conditions

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome
  • Pronator syndrome
  • Compartment syndrome
  • Colles fracture
  • Smith fracture

✋ 12. Hand

Carpal Bones

Proximal row: Scaphoid, Lunate, Triquetrum, Pisiform

Distal row: Trapezium, Trapezoid, Capitate, Hamate

Mnemonic:

Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle

Thenar Muscles

  • Abductor pollicis brevis
  • Flexor pollicis brevis
  • Opponens pollicis

Hypothenar Muscles

  • Abductor digiti minimi
  • Flexor digiti minimi
  • Opponens digiti minimi

Carpal Tunnel

The carpal tunnel contains the median nerve and long flexor tendons of the fingers and thumb.

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome:

Compression of the median nerve can cause pain, tingling and numbness in its sensory distribution and weakness of thenar muscles.

🩸 13. Arterial Supply

Main sequence:

Subclavian → Axillary → Brachial → Radial + Ulnar

Brachial Artery

The brachial artery is the continuation of the axillary artery below the lower border of teres major.

It usually terminates in the cubital fossa by dividing into radial and ulnar arteries.

Important Branches

  • Profunda brachii artery
  • Superior ulnar collateral artery
  • Inferior ulnar collateral artery
  • Radial artery
  • Ulnar artery

🩸 14. Venous Drainage

Superficial Veins

  • Cephalic vein
  • Basilic vein
  • Median cubital vein

Deep Veins

Deep veins generally accompany arteries and are called venae comitantes.

Exam Point:

The median cubital vein is clinically important for venipuncture.

🧬 15. Lymphatic Drainage

Lymph from the upper limb ultimately drains mainly toward axillary lymph nodes.

Important Axillary Groups

  • Pectoral/anterior
  • Subscapular/posterior
  • Humeral/lateral
  • Central
  • Apical

The axillary nodes are clinically important in infections and malignancy involving the upper limb and breast.

🚑 16. Important Clinical Fractures

Condition Important Point
Clavicle fracture Common fracture; middle third is frequently involved.
Surgical neck fracture May injure axillary nerve.
Midshaft humerus fracture May injure radial nerve in radial groove.
Supracondylar fracture Can threaten brachial artery and median nerve.
Colles fracture Distal radius fracture with characteristic dorsal displacement.
Scaphoid fracture Important because of risk of avascular necrosis.
Bennett fracture Fracture-dislocation involving base of first metacarpal.

🧠 17. Important Nerve Injuries

Nerve Typical Injury Clinical Finding
Axillary Surgical neck fracture Weak shoulder abduction, deltoid weakness
Radial Midshaft humerus injury Wrist drop
Median Carpal tunnel / forearm injuries Thenar weakness and sensory symptoms
Ulnar Medial epicondyle injury Weak intrinsic hand muscles, clawing
Long thoracic Chest/axillary trauma Winging of scapula

📚 18. MBBS Past-Paper Based High-Yield Questions

The following questions are adapted from recurring UHS/RMU-style upper-limb examination themes. They are paraphrased for study use.

Q1. Describe the axillary artery.

Answer:

The axillary artery is the continuation of the subclavian artery from the lateral border of the first rib. It is divided into three parts by pectoralis minor and becomes the brachial artery at the lower border of teres major.

Its branches are distributed according to its three parts: 1, 2 and 3 branches respectively.

Q2. Describe the relation of the brachial plexus to the axillary artery.

Answer:

The cords of the brachial plexus are named according to their relationship with the second part of the axillary artery: lateral cord, medial cord and posterior cord.

Q3. Describe the brachial plexus under roots, trunks, divisions and cords.

Answer:

Roots: C5-T1

Trunks: Upper, middle and lower

Divisions: Anterior and posterior

Cords: Lateral, medial and posterior

Q4. What is Erb-Duchenne palsy?

Answer:

It is classically associated with an upper trunk lesion of the brachial plexus involving C5-C6. It produces weakness of muscles supplied by affected nerves and the classical waiter's-tip posture.

Q5. What is Klumpke palsy?

Answer:

It is associated with lower trunk involvement, mainly C8-T1, and may cause weakness of intrinsic muscles of the hand with a claw-hand deformity.

Q6. Describe the boundaries and contents of cubital fossa.

Answer:

Boundaries include brachioradialis laterally, pronator teres medially and an imaginary line between the epicondyles superiorly. Important contents include the radial nerve, biceps tendon, brachial artery and median nerve.

Q7. Name the rotator cuff muscles.

Answer:

Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis — remembered by SITS.

Q8. Explain winging of scapula.

Answer:

Winging of scapula commonly results from paralysis of serratus anterior following injury to the long thoracic nerve.

Q9. Describe the superficial veins of upper limb.

Answer:

The main superficial veins are cephalic and basilic veins, with the median cubital vein connecting them in the cubital region.

Q10. What is the clinical importance of the anatomical snuffbox?

Answer:

It is an important surface landmark. The radial artery passes through it, and tenderness here after a fall may suggest scaphoid fracture.

📝 19. MBBS-Level MCQs

1. The axillary artery becomes the brachial artery at:

A. Lower border of pectoralis minor

B. Upper border of teres major

C. Lower border of teres major

D. Surgical neck of humerus

Answer: C — Lower border of teres major
2. Which nerve is most closely associated with the surgical neck of humerus?

A. Radial nerve

B. Axillary nerve

C. Median nerve

D. Ulnar nerve

Answer: B — Axillary nerve
3. Winging of scapula is classically associated with injury to:

A. Axillary nerve

B. Radial nerve

C. Long thoracic nerve

D. Musculocutaneous nerve

Answer: C — Long thoracic nerve
4. The roots of the brachial plexus are:

A. C1-C4

B. C5-T1

C. C6-T2

D. C4-T2

Answer: B — C5-T1
5. The upper trunk of brachial plexus is formed by:

A. C5-C6

B. C6-C7

C. C7-C8

D. C8-T1

Answer: A — C5-C6
6. Which nerve supplies the deltoid muscle?

A. Radial

B. Axillary

C. Median

D. Ulnar

Answer: B — Axillary nerve
7. Which muscle initiates abduction of the arm?

A. Deltoid

B. Teres major

C. Supraspinatus

D. Subscapularis

Answer: C — Supraspinatus
8. Which nerve is commonly injured in a midshaft fracture of humerus?

A. Median

B. Ulnar

C. Axillary

D. Radial

Answer: D — Radial nerve
9. Which vein is commonly used for venipuncture?

A. Cephalic vein

B. Basilic vein

C. Median cubital vein

D. Axillary vein

Answer: C — Median cubital vein
10. Which nerve passes through the carpal tunnel?

A. Ulnar

B. Radial

C. Median

D. Axillary

Answer: C — Median nerve
11. Which of the following is NOT a rotator cuff muscle?

A. Supraspinatus

B. Infraspinatus

C. Teres major

D. Subscapularis

Answer: C — Teres major
12. The posterior cord of brachial plexus gives rise to:

A. Median and ulnar nerves

B. Axillary and radial nerves

C. Musculocutaneous and median nerves

D. Ulnar and musculocutaneous nerves

Answer: B — Axillary and radial nerves
13. The main nerve of the posterior compartment of arm is:

A. Median

B. Ulnar

C. Radial

D. Musculocutaneous

Answer: C — Radial nerve
14. Which muscle is mainly responsible for elbow extension?

A. Biceps brachii

B. Brachialis

C. Triceps brachii

D. Coracobrachialis

Answer: C — Triceps brachii
15. Which nerve supplies the anterior compartment of arm?

A. Musculocutaneous

B. Radial

C. Ulnar

D. Median

Answer: A — Musculocutaneous nerve
16. Which artery is the continuation of the axillary artery?

A. Radial

B. Ulnar

C. Brachial

D. Subclavian

Answer: C — Brachial artery
17. A patient with wrist drop most likely has injury to:

A. Median nerve

B. Radial nerve

C. Ulnar nerve

D. Axillary nerve

Answer: B — Radial nerve
18. Which carpal bone is most commonly associated with avascular necrosis after fracture?

A. Pisiform

B. Hamate

C. Scaphoid

D. Trapezium

Answer: C — Scaphoid
19. Which nerve is most likely affected in medial epicondyle injury?

A. Radial

B. Axillary

C. Median

D. Ulnar

Answer: D — Ulnar nerve
20. Which structure is found in the cubital fossa?

A. Brachial artery

B. Axillary artery

C. Subclavian artery

D. Posterior circumflex humeral artery

Answer: A — Brachial artery
