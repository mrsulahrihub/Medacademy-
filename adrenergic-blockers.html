Adrenergic Blockers

MedAcademy - Medical Students

1. Introduction to Adrenergic Blockers

Adrenergic blockers are drugs that reduce the effects of sympathetic stimulation by blocking alpha (α) or beta (β) adrenergic receptors.

They are important in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia, glaucoma, heart failure, arrhythmias and several other conditions.

Main Classes:
  • Alpha-adrenergic blockers
  • Beta-adrenergic blockers
  • Mixed alpha + beta blockers

2. Alpha-Adrenergic Receptors

Receptor G Protein Important Effects
α1 Gq Vasoconstriction, mydriasis, increased sphincter tone
α2 Gi ↓ Norepinephrine release, ↓ sympathetic outflow

Most clinically important alpha blockers act mainly on α1 receptors.

3. Alpha-Adrenergic Blockers

Alpha blockers prevent catecholamines such as norepinephrine from activating alpha adrenergic receptors.

Major Types

  • Selective α1 blockers: Prazosin, Terazosin, Doxazosin, Tamsulosin
  • Nonselective alpha blockers: Phenoxybenzamine, Phentolamine

4. Selective α1 Blockers

Selective α1 blockers cause relaxation of vascular smooth muscle and smooth muscle of the prostate and bladder neck.

Examples

  • Prazosin
  • Terazosin
  • Doxazosin
  • Tamsulosin

Major Effects

  • Vasodilation
  • ↓ Peripheral vascular resistance
  • ↓ Blood pressure
  • Relaxation of prostate and bladder neck
  • Improved urinary flow in BPH

5. Prazosin

Prazosin is a selective α1-adrenergic receptor antagonist.

Mechanism

α1 blockade → vascular smooth muscle relaxation → vasodilation → ↓ peripheral resistance → ↓ blood pressure

Clinical Uses

  • Hypertension in selected patients
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia in selected cases
  • Other selected clinical applications

Adverse Effects

  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Dizziness
  • Headache
  • Weakness
  • Syncope
First-dose phenomenon: Prazosin may produce significant postural hypotension and syncope, especially after the first dose.

6. Tamsulosin

Tamsulosin preferentially blocks α1 receptors in the prostate and lower urinary tract.

Main Clinical Use

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

Mechanism in BPH

α1 blockade → relaxation of prostate and bladder-neck smooth muscle → improved urinary flow.

Advantages

Because it is relatively selective for lower urinary tract α1 receptors, it generally causes less blood-pressure lowering than nonselective alpha blockade, although dizziness and orthostatic hypotension can still occur.

Adverse Effects

  • Dizziness
  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Ejaculatory dysfunction
Exam Point: Tamsulosin → α1 blocker → BPH.

7. Nonselective Alpha Blockers

Nonselective alpha blockers inhibit both α1 and α2 receptors.

Important Drugs

  • Phenoxybenzamine
  • Phentolamine

8. Phenoxybenzamine

Phenoxybenzamine is a long-acting, irreversible, nonselective alpha-adrenergic antagonist.

Major Clinical Use

It is classically used for preoperative preparation of patients with pheochromocytoma.

Effects

  • Vasodilation
  • ↓ Blood pressure
  • Reduced effects of circulating catecholamines

Adverse Effects

  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Reflex tachycardia
  • Nasal congestion
  • Fatigue
  • Ejaculatory dysfunction
High-Yield Rule: When both alpha and beta blockade are required in pheochromocytoma, adequate alpha blockade must be established before beta blockade.

9. Phentolamine

Phentolamine is a short-acting, reversible, nonselective alpha-adrenergic antagonist.

Clinical Uses

  • Selected hypertensive emergencies related to catecholamine excess
  • Management of catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction in selected settings
  • Extravasation of certain vasoconstricting drugs

Adverse Effects

  • Hypotension
  • Reflex tachycardia
  • Palpitations

10. Beta-Adrenergic Receptors

Receptor G Protein Main Effects
β1 Gs ↑ Heart rate, ↑ contractility, ↑ renin
β2 Gs Bronchodilation, vasodilation, uterine relaxation
β3 Gs Detrusor relaxation, lipolysis
β1 blockade: ↓ Heart rate + ↓ contractility + ↓ AV conduction + ↓ renin release

11. Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

Beta blockers inhibit β adrenergic receptors and reduce the effects of catecholamines.

Main Groups

  • Nonselective β blockers: Propranolol, Nadolol, Timolol
  • β1-selective blockers: Metoprolol, Atenolol, Bisoprolol, Esmolol
  • Mixed α1 + β blockers: Carvedilol, Labetalol

12. Propranolol

Propranolol is a nonselective beta blocker that antagonizes both β1 and β2 receptors.

Effects

  • ↓ Heart rate
  • ↓ Cardiac contractility
  • ↓ AV conduction
  • ↓ Renin release

Clinical Uses

  • Hypertension in selected patients
  • Angina
  • Selected cardiac arrhythmias
  • Essential tremor
  • Migraine prevention
  • Hyperthyroidism symptom control
  • Portal hypertension in selected settings

Important Adverse Effects

  • Bradycardia
  • Hypotension
  • Fatigue
  • Bronchospasm
  • Masking of adrenergic symptoms of hypoglycemia
Nonselective beta blockers can worsen bronchospasm in susceptible patients because of β2 blockade.

13. β1-Selective Beta Blockers

β1-selective blockers preferentially block β1 receptors at usual therapeutic doses. Their selectivity is dose-dependent and not absolute.

Examples

  • Metoprolol
  • Atenolol
  • Bisoprolol
  • Esmolol

Main Effects

  • ↓ Heart rate
  • ↓ Cardiac contractility
  • ↓ AV conduction
  • ↓ Renin release
Clinical Advantage: β1-selective blockers generally have less β2-mediated bronchial effect than nonselective beta blockers, but they should still be used cautiously in patients with significant reactive airway disease.

14. Metoprolol

Metoprolol is a relatively β1-selective adrenergic blocker.

Clinical Uses

  • Hypertension
  • Angina
  • Selected arrhythmias
  • Post-myocardial infarction therapy
  • Heart failure with appropriate formulations and indications

Adverse Effects

  • Bradycardia
  • Hypotension
  • Fatigue
  • Dizziness
  • AV conduction slowing

15. Esmolol

Esmolol is a short-acting, β1-selective beta blocker.

Important Feature

It has a very short duration of action, making it useful when rapid titration of beta blockade is desired.

Clinical Uses

  • Selected supraventricular tachyarrhythmias
  • Perioperative tachycardia or hypertension in selected settings
  • Situations requiring rapidly adjustable beta blockade

16. Carvedilol

Carvedilol blocks β receptors and also blocks α1 receptors.

Effects

  • ↓ Heart rate
  • ↓ Cardiac contractility
  • ↓ Sympathetic activity
  • α1 blockade → vasodilation

Clinical Use

Carvedilol is an important evidence-based beta blocker for selected patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

Remember: Carvedilol → β blockade + α1 blockade.

17. Labetalol

Labetalol blocks both α1 and β adrenergic receptors.

Clinical Uses

  • Hypertensive emergencies in selected settings
  • Severe hypertension associated with pregnancy
  • Other situations where combined alpha and beta blockade is useful

Important Effect

α1 blockade produces vasodilation while β blockade reduces cardiac stimulation and limits reflex tachycardia.

18. Clinical Uses of Adrenergic Blockers

Condition Important Drugs
Hypertension Selected alpha blockers, beta blockers, labetalol
Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia Tamsulosin, doxazosin, terazosin
Heart Failure Carvedilol, selected evidence-based β1-selective blockers
Angina Beta blockers
Selected Arrhythmias Metoprolol, esmolol, propranolol
Glaucoma Timolol
Pheochromocytoma Phenoxybenzamine; other alpha blockade strategies
Hypertension in pregnancy Labetalol in appropriate clinical settings
Essential Tremor Propranolol
Migraine Prevention Propranolol in selected patients

19. Adverse Effects of Alpha Blockers

  • Orthostatic hypotension
  • Dizziness
  • Syncope
  • Headache
  • Nasal congestion
  • Reflex tachycardia, especially with nonselective alpha blockade
  • Fatigue
  • Ejaculatory dysfunction with some agents
High-Yield: α1 blockade → vasodilation → orthostatic hypotension. Nonselective alpha blockade can also cause reflex tachycardia.

20. Adverse Effects of Beta Blockers

  • Bradycardia
  • Hypotension
  • Fatigue
  • Dizziness
  • AV conduction abnormalities
  • Bronchospasm with nonselective agents
  • Masking of adrenergic symptoms of hypoglycemia
  • Cold extremities
  • Sexual dysfunction in some patients
Beta blockers should generally not be stopped abruptly after prolonged therapy because rebound sympathetic activity may cause tachycardia, hypertension or worsening angina in susceptible patients.

21. Important Cautions with Beta Blockers

Beta blockers require particular caution or may be contraindicated in certain clinical situations.

  • Severe bradycardia
  • Significant heart block without appropriate pacing
  • Cardiogenic shock or acute decompensated heart failure in certain settings
  • Severe reactive airway disease, especially with nonselective agents

The exact contraindications depend on the individual beta blocker and clinical condition.

22. Alpha Blockers vs Beta Blockers

Feature Alpha Blockers Beta Blockers
Main receptor α receptors β receptors
Heart rate May increase reflexively Usually decreases
Vascular tone Decreases Variable depending on drug
Blood pressure Generally decreases Generally decreases
Bronchial effect Minimal direct effect Nonselective blockers may cause bronchoconstriction
BPH Important role Not primary therapy
Heart failure Not standard therapy Selected beta blockers are important

24. Adrenergic Blockade in Pheochromocytoma

Pheochromocytoma is a catecholamine-secreting tumor that can cause severe hypertension and other sympathetic symptoms.

Important Principle

Alpha blockade is established first when both alpha and beta blockade are needed.

Why? Giving a beta blocker first can leave α1-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed and may cause severe hypertension.

Classic Drug

Phenoxybenzamine is a classic long-acting alpha blocker used for preoperative preparation.

25. Beta Blockers in Glaucoma

Some beta blockers reduce aqueous humor production and therefore lower intraocular pressure.

Timolol

Timolol is a nonselective beta blocker used topically in glaucoma.

Even topical beta blockers can have systemic effects such as bradycardia or bronchospasm.

26. High-Yield Revision Points

  • α1 receptor blockade causes vasodilation.
  • Prazosin is a selective α1 blocker.
  • Prazosin can cause first-dose orthostatic hypotension.
  • Tamsulosin is preferentially used for BPH.
  • Phenoxybenzamine is an irreversible nonselective alpha blocker.
  • Phenoxybenzamine is classically used for preoperative pheochromocytoma management.
  • Phentolamine is a short-acting reversible nonselective alpha blocker.
  • Propranolol blocks β1 and β2 receptors.
  • Metoprolol is relatively β1-selective.
  • Esmolol is a very short-acting β1-selective blocker.
  • Timolol is a nonselective beta blocker used in glaucoma.
  • Carvedilol blocks α1 and β receptors.
  • Labetalol blocks α1 and β receptors.
  • Nonselective beta blockers may cause bronchospasm.
  • Beta blockers decrease heart rate and contractility.
  • Beta blockers may mask adrenergic symptoms of hypoglycemia.
  • In pheochromocytoma, establish alpha blockade before beta blockade when both are needed.

27. Important MCQs

Q1. Which receptor is blocked by prazosin?

  1. α1
  2. α2
  3. β1
  4. β2

Answer: A — α1

Q2. Which drug is commonly used for BPH?

  1. Phenylephrine
  2. Tamsulosin
  3. Dobutamine
  4. Epinephrine

Answer: B — Tamsulosin

Q3. Which alpha blocker is classically used in pheochromocytoma?

  1. Phenoxybenzamine
  2. Metoprolol
  3. Salbutamol
  4. Timolol

Answer: A — Phenoxybenzamine

Q4. What is a characteristic adverse effect of prazosin?

  1. Severe bronchospasm
  2. First-dose orthostatic hypotension
  3. Hyperglycemia
  4. Marked miosis

Answer: B — First-dose orthostatic hypotension

Q5. Which drug is a nonselective beta blocker?

  1. Metoprolol
  2. Propranolol
  3. Esmolol
  4. Bisoprolol

Answer: B — Propranolol

Q6. Which beta blocker is relatively β1-selective?

  1. Propranolol
  2. Timolol
  3. Metoprolol
  4. Nadolol

Answer: C — Metoprolol

Q7. Which drug is very short acting and β1-selective?

  1. Esmolol
  2. Propranolol
  3. Timolol
  4. Phenoxybenzamine

Answer: A — Esmolol

Q8. Which drug blocks both α1 and β receptors?

  1. Carvedilol
  2. Propranolol
  3. Metoprolol
  4. Tamsulosin

Answer: A — Carvedilol

Q9. Which beta blocker is commonly used topically in glaucoma?

  1. Timolol
  2. Prazosin
  3. Phenoxybenzamine
  4. Labetalol

Answer: A — Timolol

Q10. Which beta blocker may cause bronchospasm due to β2 blockade?

  1. Propranolol
  2. Metoprolol
  3. Bisoprolol
  4. Esmolol

Answer: A — Propranolol

Q11. In pheochromocytoma, which blockade should be established first when both are needed?

  1. Beta blockade
  2. Alpha blockade
  3. Muscarinic blockade
  4. Calcium-channel blockade

Answer: B — Alpha blockade

Q12. Why should beta blockade not be started first in pheochromocytoma?

  1. It causes severe hypoglycemia in every patient
  2. It may leave α1-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed
  3. It blocks muscarinic receptors
  4. It directly stimulates alpha receptors

Answer: B — It may leave α1-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed

Q13. Which drug has both alpha and beta blocking activity and is used in selected hypertensive emergencies?

  1. Labetalol
  2. Salbutamol
  3. Pilocarpine
  4. Phenylephrine

Answer: A — Labetalol

Q14. Which beta blocker is important in selected patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction?

  1. Carvedilol
  2. Phenylephrine
  3. Prazosin
  4. Phentolamine

Answer: A — Carvedilol

Q15. Beta blockers primarily reduce:

  1. Heart rate and cardiac contractility
  2. Bronchial smooth muscle tone only
  3. Salivation
  4. Gastric acid secretion only

Answer: A — Heart rate and cardiac contractility
