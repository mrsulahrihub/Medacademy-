Introduction to the Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is a part of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary physiological functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, digestion, pupil size, glandular secretion and smooth muscle activity.

Key Concept:

ANS mainly controls:

Heart → Blood vessels → Smooth muscle → Glands → Internal organs

Autonomic pharmacology focuses on drugs that stimulate, inhibit or modify the actions of autonomic neurotransmitters and their receptors.

1. Divisions of the Autonomic Nervous System

The ANS is traditionally divided into the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems. The enteric nervous system is also an important component of autonomic regulation.

Division Main Role Typical Effects
Sympathetic "Fight or flight" Increased heart rate, pupil dilation, reduced gastrointestinal activity
Parasympathetic "Rest and digest" Reduced heart rate, increased digestion, pupil constriction
Enteric Controls gastrointestinal function Regulates intestinal motility and secretion

2. Basic Autonomic Pathway

Most autonomic pathways involve two neurons between the central nervous system and the target organ.

CNS → Preganglionic Neuron → Autonomic Ganglion → Postganglionic Neuron → Effector Organ

Preganglionic Neurons

Both sympathetic and parasympathetic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine (ACh).

Postganglionic Neurons

Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release ACh, whereas most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine (NE).

Important Exception:

Sympathetic postganglionic fibers supplying most sweat glands release acetylcholine rather than norepinephrine.

3. Major Autonomic Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitter Major Location
Acetylcholine (ACh) All autonomic preganglionic neurons, most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons, and sympathetic sweat glands
Norepinephrine (NE) Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons
Epinephrine Mainly released into blood from the adrenal medulla

4. Cholinergic Receptors

Cholinergic receptors respond to acetylcholine. They are divided into two major groups: muscarinic and nicotinic receptors.

Nicotinic Receptors

  • NN receptors: Located mainly in autonomic ganglia and adrenal medulla.
  • NM receptors: Located at the neuromuscular junction of skeletal muscle.

Muscarinic Receptors

  • M1: Important in neural and gastric functions.
  • M2: Important in the heart; activation decreases heart rate.
  • M3: Important in glands and smooth muscle.
High-Yield:

M2 → Heart
M3 → Glands and smooth muscle

5. Adrenergic Receptors

Adrenergic receptors respond mainly to norepinephrine and epinephrine.

Major Receptor Types

Receptor Major Effects
α1 Vasoconstriction, mydriasis and contraction of some smooth muscles
α2 Decreases presynaptic neurotransmitter release; reduces sympathetic outflow when activated centrally
β1 Increased heart rate, contractility and renin release
β2 Bronchodilation and relaxation of certain smooth muscles
β3 Lipolysis and relaxation of the detrusor muscle

6. Adrenergic Receptors and G-Proteins

Receptor G-Protein Main Signaling Effect
α1 Gq ↑ IP3 / DAG → ↑ intracellular Ca2+
α2 Gi ↓ cAMP
β1 Gs ↑ cAMP
β2 Gs ↑ cAMP
β3 Gs ↑ cAMP
Memory Trick:

α1 → Gq
α2 → Gi
β1, β2, β3 → Gs

7. Sympathetic vs Parasympathetic Effects

Organ Sympathetic Parasympathetic
Heart ↑ Heart rate and contractility ↓ Heart rate
Pupil Dilation Constriction
Bronchi Dilatation Constriction
Gastrointestinal tract Reduced motility and secretion Increased motility and secretion
Bladder Relaxes detrusor and promotes sphincter contraction Contracts detrusor and promotes urination
Salivary glands Reduced volume, more viscous secretion Increased watery secretion

8. Cholinergic Agonists

Cholinergic agonists, also called parasympathomimetics, mimic the effects of acetylcholine.

Direct-Acting Agonists

  • Bethanechol
  • Pilocarpine
  • Carbachol

Major Effects

  • Increased glandular secretion.
  • Increased gastrointestinal motility.
  • Increased bladder contraction.
  • Miosis.
  • Reduced heart rate.
Clinical Example:

Bethanechol may be used to stimulate bladder contraction in selected cases of urinary retention.

9. Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors reduce the breakdown of acetylcholine and therefore increase cholinergic activity.

Examples

  • Neostigmine
  • Pyridostigmine
  • Physostigmine
  • Donepezil
  • Rivastigmine
Clinical Examples:

Pyridostigmine is used in myasthenia gravis.

Donepezil is used in the management of Alzheimer's disease.

10. Antimuscarinic Drugs

Antimuscarinic drugs block muscarinic receptors and reduce parasympathetic effects.

Examples

  • Atropine
  • Scopolamine
  • Ipratropium
  • Tiotropium
  • Oxybutynin

Common Effects

  • Increased heart rate.
  • Mydriasis.
  • Reduced salivary secretion.
  • Reduced gastrointestinal motility.
  • Reduced bladder contraction.
  • Bronchodilation.
Classic Anticholinergic Effects:

Dry mouth + Mydriasis + Tachycardia + Reduced GI motility + Urinary retention

11. Adrenergic Agonists

Adrenergic agonists, also called sympathomimetics, stimulate adrenergic receptors.

Important Drugs

Drug Main Receptor Activity Important Use
Epinephrine α and β receptors Anaphylaxis and other emergency indications
Phenylephrine α1 Nasal decongestion and selected hypotensive states
Salbutamol β2 Bronchodilation
Dobutamine Mainly β1 Selected cases of acute cardiac dysfunction

12. Adrenergic Antagonists

Adrenergic antagonists block the actions of catecholamines at adrenergic receptors.

Alpha Blockers

  • Prazosin
  • Tamsulosin
  • Phenoxybenzamine

Beta Blockers

  • Propranolol
  • Metoprolol
  • Atenolol
  • Carvedilol
Clinical Example:

Tamsulosin selectively blocks α1A receptors and can help improve urinary flow in benign prostatic hyperplasia.

13. Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

Beta blockers reduce the effects of beta-adrenergic stimulation.

Major Effects

  • Reduced heart rate.
  • Reduced cardiac contractility.
  • Reduced renin release.
  • Reduced blood pressure in appropriate patients.
Important:

Non-selective beta blockers can block β2 receptors and may cause bronchoconstriction in susceptible patients.
Clinical Examples:

Beta blockers may be used in conditions such as hypertension, angina, certain arrhythmias and selected heart failure patients.

14. Important Clinical Applications

Drug / Class Clinical Application
Epinephrine Anaphylaxis
Salbutamol Acute relief of bronchospasm
Ipratropium / Tiotropium Obstructive airway disease
Atropine Selected cases of symptomatic bradycardia
Pyridostigmine Myasthenia gravis
Propranolol Selected cardiovascular and other conditions
Tamsulosin Benign prostatic hyperplasia

15. High-Yield Receptor Summary

Receptor G-Protein / Channel Major Location / Effect
M1 Gq Neural and gastric functions
M2 Gi Heart → ↓ heart rate
M3 Gq Glands and smooth muscle
Nicotinic Ligand-gated ion channel Autonomic ganglia and neuromuscular junction
α1 Gq Vasoconstriction

β1 Gs Heart and kidney
β2 Gs Bronchodilation
β3 Gs Detrusor relaxation

16. Quick Revision Points

  • Sympathetic system = fight or flight.
  • Parasympathetic system = rest and digest.
  • All autonomic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine.
  • Most parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release ACh.
  • Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine.
  • Sympathetic sweat glands are an important cholinergic exception.
  • M2 receptors are important in the heart.
  • M3 receptors are important in glands and smooth muscle.
  • α1 = Gq.
  • α2 = Gi.
  • β1, β2 and β3 = Gs.
  • β1 stimulation increases heart rate and contractility.
  • β2 stimulation produces bronchodilation.
  • Antimuscarinic drugs block muscarinic receptors.
  • Beta blockers reduce beta-adrenergic effects.

17. Autonomic Nervous System - Important MCQs

Q1. The sympathetic nervous system is commonly associated with:

A. Rest and digest
B. Fight or flight
C. Sleep only
D. Digestion only

Answer: B. Fight or flight
Q2. The neurotransmitter released by all autonomic preganglionic neurons is:

A. Dopamine
B. Norepinephrine
C. Acetylcholine
D. Serotonin

Answer: C. Acetylcholine
Q3. Which receptor is associated with Gq?

A. α1
B. α2
C. β1
D. β2

Answer: A. α1
Q4. Which receptor is most important for parasympathetic effects on the heart?

A. M1
B. M2
C. M3
D. β1

Answer: B. M2
Q5. β2 receptor stimulation causes:

A. Bronchoconstriction
B. Bronchodilation
C. Bradycardia
D. Miosis

Answer: B. Bronchodilation
Q6. Which drug is a muscarinic antagonist?

A. Pilocarpine
B. Bethanechol
C. Atropine
D. Pyridostigmine

Answer: C. Atropine
Q7. Which drug is a β2 agonist?

A. Salbutamol
B. Atropine
C. Propranolol
D. Prazosin

Answer: A. Salbutamol
Q8. Pyridostigmine is an:

A. Alpha blocker
B. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor
C. Beta blocker
D. Muscarinic antagonist

Answer: B. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor
Q9. Which receptor is associated with Gi?

A. α1
B. α2
C. β1
D. β2

Answer: B. α2
Q10. Which drug is commonly used as an emergency treatment for anaphylaxis?

A. Epinephrine
B. Tamsulosin
C. Atropine
D. Propranolol

Answer: A. Epinephrine
